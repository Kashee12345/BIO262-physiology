The Respiratory System

Moving air is mechanics. Moving gas is diffusion. They are two different problems.

By the end of this page you should be able to
  • Explain how a pressure difference moves air in and out
  • Say what surfactant does, and why a premature baby struggles without it
  • Explain gas exchange using pressure differences
  • Describe how oxygen and carbon dioxide each travel in blood
  • Read the oxygen curve and predict what shifts it

1Two zones, two jobs

Most of the airway does no gas exchange at all.

The conducting zone is the nose, windpipe, and branching tubes. It warms, moistens, and filters air, then delivers it. No exchange happens here, which is why it is called dead space.

The respiratory zone is the air sacs. Every bit of gas exchange happens there.

That surface is huge and the barrier is thin: one air sac cell and one capillary cell. Anything that thickens the barrier, like fluid, infection, or scarring, slows diffusion and shows up as shortness of breath.

2Breathing is a pressure problem

  1. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, and the ribs lift. The chest gets bigger.
  2. Bigger space means lower pressure inside the lungs, now below the pressure outside.
  3. Air flows in, because gas moves from higher pressure to lower. That is all breathing in is.
  4. The diaphragm relaxes, the chest springs back, space shrinks, and pressure rises.
  5. Air flows out. Quiet breathing out is passive, so it costs nothing.

The lungs do not pull air in. They are inflated by a pressure difference the chest wall creates around them, and they stay stuck to that chest wall because of the sealed space between them.

Why the nurse cares

Break that seal with a chest wound and air gets into the space. The lung is no longer held open against the chest wall, so it collapses. Nothing is wrong with the lung itself. The thing holding it open is gone.

Surfactant is why small air sacs stay open. A wet surface pulls itself closed, and the smaller the bubble, the harder it pulls. Surfactant reduces that pull, so air sacs do not collapse at the end of each breath and do not have to be reopened from scratch. Premature babies may not make enough yet, which is exactly why their breathing is so hard.

3Gas exchange costs no energy at all

Every gas moves from where there is more of it to where there is less. Nothing pumps it.

WhereOxygenCarbon dioxideNet movement
Air sac vs arriving bloodHigh in the air sacHigh in the bloodOxygen into blood, carbon dioxide into the air sac
Blood vs working tissueHigh in the bloodHigh in the tissueOxygen into tissue, carbon dioxide into blood

It is the same rule in both places. The direction flips only because the difference flips. Learn the rule and you never have to memorize the direction.

4How each gas actually travels

  • Oxygen. About 98% is carried on hemoglobin, and the rest is dissolved. The dissolved part is small, but it is what the pressure reading measures, and it is what drives loading and unloading.
  • Carbon dioxide. Most of it is turned into bicarbonate inside the red cell. Some rides on hemoglobin, and a little is dissolved.

That bicarbonate route links breathing straight to acid balance. Carbon dioxide plus water makes an acid, which splits into bicarbonate and a hydrogen ion.

So holding on to carbon dioxide builds up hydrogen ions and makes the blood acidic. Blowing carbon dioxide off does the opposite.

That is why a patient who breathes too little becomes acidic, why one who over-breathes becomes alkaline, and why the lungs can correct a body-wide acid problem in minutes just by changing how hard the patient breathes.

5The curve, and why it bends

Move the tissue conditions and watch how much oxygen gets released.

Oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation

The curve is S-shaped for a reason.

The flat top means that in the lungs, oxygen loading stays high even if the oxygen in the air drops somewhat. That is a built-in safety margin.

The steep bottom means that in the tissues, a small drop in oxygen releases a lot of it.

The curve also shifts. Working tissue is hot, acidic, and full of carbon dioxide, and every one of those makes hemoglobin let go of oxygen more easily.

So the tissue that needs oxygen most changes its own local conditions in the way that delivers it. There is no signal and no control center. The chemistry does it.

Think of it this way

Hemoglobin holds oxygen loosely in a hot, acidic crowd and tightly in a cool, calm one. Exercising muscle makes itself exactly the kind of place where hemoglobin lets go.
