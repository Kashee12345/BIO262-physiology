The Endocrine System

The body's second messaging service. Slower than nerves, but it reaches everywhere and it lasts.

By the end of this page you should be able to
  • Compare nerves and hormones on speed, reach, and how long they last
  • Explain why a hormone in the blood only affects certain cells
  • Say why steroid and peptide hormones act on different parts of the cell
  • Trace a hormone chain from brain to gland, and predict what feedback does
  • Name the major glands and what too much or too little of each looks like

1Two messaging systems, one body

ReachWherever the nerve endsEverywhere blood goes
How longBriefSustained
Good forPulling your hand off a stoveGrowth, metabolism, reproduction, fluid balance

The two overlap on purpose. The sympathetic nerves fire in a second and are done in a second. So the adrenal gland adds epinephrine to the blood and holds the response open for minutes.

The fast system starts it. The slow system keeps it going.

2Why a hormone in every vessel does not affect every cell

Delivery is universal. Response is not.

A hormone reaches every cell in the body. But only cells with the matching receptor can respond. Everything else sits in the signal and ignores it.

That is the whole basis of specificity. It also explains a lot of disease. A tissue can lose its receptors and stop responding, even when hormone levels are perfectly normal.

Think of it this way

The hormone is an announcement over the whole building. Only people holding the right badge are supposed to act. Take away the badges and the announcement changes nothing, however loud it gets.

Where the receptor sits depends on the hormone. Steroid hormones dissolve in fat, so they pass straight through the membrane and bind receptors inside the cell. They change which genes are read, so they are slow to start and long to last. Peptide hormones cannot cross the membrane, so they bind receptors on the surface and set off a messenger inside. They are fast to start and short to last. Insulin is a peptide. Cortisol is a steroid. That is why one acts in minutes and the other over hours.

3A hormone chain, and what feedback does to it

Move the hormone level and watch the whole chain respond.

Hypothalamus → pituitary → thyroid, with feedback

  1. The hypothalamus releases a starter hormone into a short local circulation.
  2. The pituitary answers by releasing a stimulating hormone into the bloodstream.
  3. The target gland — thyroid, adrenal, or gonad — releases the final hormone.
  4. That final hormone feeds back and switches off the first two steps. Negative feedback again, exactly as in Module 1.
Why the nurse cares

A patient on long-term steroids has a shut-down chain. The pituitary stopped signaling, and the adrenal gland shrank. Stop the drug suddenly and there is no hormone from either source. That is why steroids are tapered, never stopped all at once.

5Insulin and glucagon — the clearest loop in the body

Two hormones from one organ, pulling opposite ways on one value.

After a meal, blood sugar rises and the pancreas releases insulin. Insulin opens the door for sugar to enter muscle and fat, and tells the liver to store the extra. Blood sugar falls back to normal.

Between meals, blood sugar falls and the pancreas releases glucagon instead. The liver breaks down its stores and puts sugar back into the blood. Blood sugar rises to normal.

Now think about what happens with no insulin, or with insulin that is ignored. Sugar stays in the blood and cannot get into the cells that need it.

So blood sugar is high while the cells act starved. That contradiction — sugar everywhere, fuel nowhere — is the core of diabetes. It explains the thirst, the urinating, and the weight loss all at once.
