The Brain & the Autonomic Nervous System

Where the control centers sit, and the two settings the body runs on.

By the end of this page you should be able to
  • Match each brain region to the job it controls
  • Say why brainstem damage is more deadly than damage higher up
  • Trace a reflex arc, and explain why it does not wait for the brain
  • Compare the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems, organ by organ
  • Name the chemicals of each side, and predict what a drug that blocks them will do

1The map that matters

You do not need every fold of the brain. You need to know which part failed when a patient changes.

RegionWhat it doesWhat its loss looks like
Cerebral cortexThinking, movement you choose, sensation, speechWeakness, numbness, speech and personality changes
CerebellumCoordination, balance, smooth movementClumsy and unsteady, but strength is normal
HypothalamusTemperature, hunger, thirst, hormones, set pointsFever, fluid problems, hormone problems
ThalamusRelay station for almost all sensationWidespread sensory problems
BrainstemBreathing, heart rate, blood pressure, being awakeStopped breathing, collapse, coma

Notice the pattern. Damage high up costs you abilities. Damage low down costs you life.

The brainstem is small and plain, and it runs the machinery that can never pause.

Why the nurse cares

The hypothalamus holds the set point for the loops in Module 1. A fever is not a broken thermostat. It is the thermostat set higher on purpose. That is why the patient shivers on the way up.

2The reflex arc — a decision without the brain

Some responses cannot wait for the trip upstairs.

  1. A receptor notices the stimulus. Heat, stretch, or pain.
  2. A sensory neuron carries the signal into the spinal cord.
  3. The cord decides. Sometimes across a single synapse.
  4. A motor neuron carries the command straight back out.
  5. The muscle acts. Only then does the brain find out.

You pull your hand off a hot pan before you feel the burn. The brain is told at the same time, but it is not asked.

Testing a reflex tests one small segment of cord and its nerves on their own. That is exactly why reflexes are so good at locating an injury.

3Two settings, and both are always on

Drag the dial and watch the whole body change together.

The autonomic dial

Neither side is ever fully off. What changes is the balance.

Every organ shifts at once, because the same outflow reaches all of them. That is why a frightened patient has a fast heart, wide pupils, a dry mouth, and a quiet gut all at the same time. One switch, many organs.

Think of it this way

It is not an on/off switch. It is a mixing desk with two sliders. Push one up and pull the other down, and you get the same result either way.

4The chemistry of the two sides

Learn the chemicals, and you can predict what any autonomic drug will do.

SympatheticParasympathetic
Chemical at the organNorepinephrine, mostlyAcetylcholine
Receptor thereAdrenergic: alpha and betaMuscarinic
Backup in the bloodEpinephrine from the adrenal glandNone
Main nerve routeOut of the chest and lower backThe vagus nerve, and the sacral nerves

Now the useful part.

A beta blocker blocks the sympathetic effect on the heart. So heart rate and force fall.

An anticholinergic blocks the parasympathetic side everywhere. So the heart speeds up, the pupils widen, the mouth dries, and the gut slows.

Those side-effect lists are not memorization. They are one mechanism, read across several organs.

The adrenal gland is a modified sympathetic nerve ending. Instead of touching an organ, it dumps epinephrine into the blood. That is why a fright outlasts the moment that caused it. The nerve signal ends in a second, but the hormone circulates for minutes.

5Protection, and why some drugs cannot get in

The brain is guarded by three layers, a fluid cushion, and a wall of cells.

The meninges wrap it. Cerebrospinal fluid floats it, so it does not crush under its own weight. And the blood–brain barrier controls what leaves the blood.

Capillaries elsewhere in the body are leaky on purpose. In the brain they are sealed shut.

That barrier keeps toxins out. It also keeps many useful drugs out. This is why a brain infection needs antibiotics chosen for their ability to cross it, and why brain drugs are hard to design.
