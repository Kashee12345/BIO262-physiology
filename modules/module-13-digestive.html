The Digestive System

A tube that breaks food into pieces small enough to cross a membrane. Nothing counts until it does.

By the end of this page you should be able to
  • List the six jobs the digestive system does
  • Say which nutrient is broken down where, and by what
  • Explain why the small intestine does nearly all the absorbing
  • Explain what bile does, and why it is not an enzyme
  • Say why everything absorbed from the gut goes to the liver first

1Six jobs, one tube

Food inside the digestive tract is still, technically, outside the body.

The tract is a tube running through you, open at both ends. Nothing in it has entered your body until it crosses the lining and reaches the blood.

That one idea explains the whole design. Everything before absorption exists to make absorption possible.

  1. Ingestion. Taking food in.
  2. Propulsion. Moving it along.
  3. Mechanical digestion. Chewing and churning. Breaking things up without changing the chemistry.
  4. Chemical digestion. Enzymes splitting large molecules into small ones.
  5. Absorption. Nutrients crossing into blood or lymph. This is the point of the whole thing.
  6. Elimination. Getting rid of what is left.

Two kinds of movement, and they are not the same. Peristalsis is a traveling wave that moves contents along. Segmentation is local squeezing that mixes contents with enzymes and pushes them against the wall, without moving them onward. The small intestine spends most of its time on segmentation, because absorption takes time.

3Why the small intestine does the absorbing

Surface area, and a lot of it.

Three levels of folding multiply the lining. There are folds in the wall, finger-like villi on those folds, and a brush of tiny microvilli on every cell of every villus.

The result is an absorbing surface far larger than the organ that holds it.

Inside each villus sits a network of capillaries and one lymph vessel. Sugars and amino acids go into the capillary and travel in blood. Digested fats are too big and too greasy for that route, so they are packaged and enter the lymph vessel instead. They reach the bloodstream only near the heart.

Why the nurse cares

Diseases that flatten the villi, such as celiac disease, do not block digestion. The enzymes still work fine. The patient becomes malnourished because the surface for absorbing the results is gone.

4Bile, and the problem with fat

Fat does not dissolve in water, and enzymes can only work at a surface.

One large fat droplet in watery gut contents gives lipase very little surface to attack.

Bile, made by the liver and stored in the gallbladder, breaks that droplet into many tiny ones. That hugely increases the surface, without changing a single chemical bond.

So bile is an emulsifier, not an enzyme. It does not digest anything. It makes fat digestible by lipase, which does.

Remove the gallbladder and bile still reaches the intestine from the liver. It just arrives steadily instead of in a well-timed squirt, which is why big fatty meals get harder to handle.

Think of it this way

Washing a greasy pan. The soap does not eat the grease. It breaks it into droplets small enough for the water to carry off. Bile is doing that job.

5Everything goes to the liver first

Blood leaving the intestine does not rejoin the general circulation. It is routed through the liver.

That route carries nutrient-rich blood from the gut straight to the liver, which checks it before the rest of the body sees it. The liver stores extra sugar, converts amino acids, processes drugs and toxins, and makes plasma proteins and clotting factors.

This is why a pill can be largely broken down before it ever reaches its target, and why the same drug given by injection can be far stronger at the same dose.

It is also why liver failure causes so many different problems. Nutrients, drug clearance, clotting, and fluid balance all pass through one organ.

The large intestine finishes the job with far less drama. It absorbs water and some salts, houses bacteria that make a few vitamins, and forms and stores the leftovers. It is a water-recovery operation, which is why upsetting it causes either dehydration or constipation.
