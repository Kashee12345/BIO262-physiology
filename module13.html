Journey down the alimentary canal

Tap an organ. For each one, ask the same three questions: what breaks food up physically, what breaks it down chemically, and what actually gets absorbed here.

The enzyme table

If you learn one table for this module, learn this one. Every enzyme: where it's made, where it works, what it acts on, and what it produces.

Enzyme / secretionMade byActs inSubstrate → product

Note the trap: bile is not an enzyme. It emulsifies fat — a mechanical job — so that pancreatic lipase (a real enzyme) can chemically digest it.

Accessory organs

None of these touch food directly. All of them are essential.

OrganWhat it contributesExam hook
TongueMechanical: manipulates the bolus and initiates swallowing. Sensory: taste. Chemical: secretes lingual lipase.Lingual lipase is secreted in the mouth but activated in the stomach.
TeethMastication — the first step of mechanical digestion. Incisors cut, canines tear, premolars and molars grind.Increases surface area so enzymes can work faster later.
Salivary glandsParotid, submandibular, sublingual. Secrete salivary amylase, mucins for lubrication, bicarbonate to buffer pH, and lysozyme + IgA as antibacterials.Salivary amylase starts carbohydrate digestion — the only chemical digestion in the mouth.
LiverProduces bile (bile salts, bilirubin, cholesterol). Processes absorbed nutrients, stores glucose as glycogen, detoxifies, and makes albumin and clotting factors.The liver produces bile; it does not store it.
GallbladderStores and concentrates bile, then releases it into the duodenum via the common bile duct when fat arrives.The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile. Released on the CCK signal.
PancreasExocrine: amylase, lipase, proteases (trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, procarboxypeptidase), nucleases, bicarbonate. Endocrine: insulin and glucagon.Produces most of the enzymes used in the small intestine — and the only ones that digest all three macronutrients.
Why proteases are released inactive: trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen and procarboxypeptidase are secreted in inactive form so the pancreas doesn't digest itself. They're activated once they reach the small intestine.

The four GI hormones

Each one is triggered by something specific arriving somewhere specific. Learn the trigger and the target.

HormoneReleased byTriggerEffect
GastrinStomach (G cells)Food entering the stomach; stretchStimulates gastric acid (HCl) secretion and gastric motility.
CCK
cholecystokinin		DuodenumFat and protein in the duodenumContracts the gallbladder to release bile; stimulates pancreatic enzyme secretion; slows gastric emptying.
SecretinDuodenumAcidic chyme arriving from the stomachStimulates the pancreas to release bicarbonate, neutralizing the acid.
Insulin & glucagonPancreas (beta / alpha cells)High / low blood glucoseInsulin moves glucose into cells and stores it as glycogen. Glucagon breaks glycogen back down to raise blood glucose.

Mnemonic: CCK → Contracts the gallbladder (fat trigger). Secretin → neutralizes with bicarbonate (acid trigger).

Who absorbs what

A favorite exam target. The three regions of the small intestine do different jobs.

RegionAbsorbs
StomachVery little — water, alcohol, and some drugs such as aspirin.
DuodenumIron, calcium, magnesium. Most chemical digestion happens here.
JejunumThe main absorptive site — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and water-soluble vitamins (B and C).
IleumVitamin B12 (bound to intrinsic factor), bile salts, and fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K.
Large intestineWater and electrolytes. Gut bacteria synthesize vitamin K and some B vitamins. No enzymes of its own.
Intrinsic factor: a glycoprotein made by the parietal cells of the stomach. It binds vitamin B12 so it can be absorbed in the ileum. Lose the parietal cells and you get pernicious anemia — the stomach problem that shows up as a blood problem. Tap any highlighted word to hide/show it.
