High-yield facts

  • Osteoblasts build. Osteoclasts carve.
  • PTH raises blood calcium; calcitonin lowers it.
  • Endochondral (cartilage model) → long bones. Intramembranous (membrane) → flat bones.
  • Bone is ~2/3 calcium phosphate (hardness) and ~1/3 collagen (tensile strength).

Topic walkthrough — read this first

Two bone shapes, two jobs

Flat bones are built like a sandwich — compact bone, spongy diploe, compact bone. That structure is light and impact-absorbing, which suits protection: skull, sternum, ribs.

Long bones are hollow tubes with a marrow cavity down the middle. A tube resists bending nearly as well as a solid rod at a fraction of the weight, which suits their job as levers for movement.

Bone also does two things students forget: red marrow makes blood cells, and the mineral matrix is the body's calcium bank.

Four cells, one balance

Osteoprogenitor cells are the stem cells. Osteoblasts build matrix. When an osteoblast gets walled into the matrix it made, it becomes an osteocyte, sitting in a pocket called a lacuna and staying in touch with its neighbors through tiny channels called canaliculi. Osteoclasts carve bone away and release calcium into the blood.

Remodeling is these last two working against each other for life. Tip the balance toward osteoblasts and bone thickens; tip it toward osteoclasts and bone thins. That single idea explains why loading a bone strengthens it, why bed rest weakens it, and why osteoporosis happens after menopause when falling estrogen releases the brake on osteoclasts.

Two ways to make a bone

Endochondral ossification lays down a hyaline cartilage model first and replaces it with bone. It makes long bones, and it leaves behind the epiphyseal plate — the cartilage growth zone that lets a bone lengthen until it ossifies at maturity.

Intramembranous ossification skips cartilage entirely and forms bone directly within a fibrous membrane. It makes flat bones.

Calcium is a blood value, not a bone value

The body does not regulate bone; it regulates blood calcium, and bone is the reserve it raids. When blood calcium falls, PTH is released and Pulls calcium into the blood — stimulating osteoclasts, increasing gut absorption via vitamin D, and reducing losses in urine. When blood calcium rises, calcitonin tones it down by inhibiting osteoclasts and favoring deposition. Two hormones, opposite directions, one narrow target range.

How bones get longer, and how they get thicker

A long bone lengthens and thickens by different mechanisms.

Length comes from the epiphyseal plate, five zones of cartilage. The resting zone anchors it to the epiphysis; in the proliferative zone chondrocytes divide into columns that shove the epiphysis away; in the hypertrophic zone they swell; in the calcification zone the hardening matrix starves them; and in the ossification zone osteoblasts lay bone over them. Cartilage is added above as fast as it is replaced below, so the plate never thins.

Width comes from appositional growth — osteoblasts under the periosteum add bone outside while osteoclasts widen the marrow cavity within. Until sex steroids close the plate at puberty's end it is that bone's only source of length, which is why a child's growth-plate injury can shorten the limb for life.

Remodeling, and why load decides where bone goes

Bone is not inert — about a tenth of your skeleton is dismantled and rebuilt every year.

The cycle runs in a fixed order. Osteocytes trapped in the matrix sense strain and microcracks and call for turnover. Osteoclasts arrive and dissolve mineral with acid and matrix with enzymes, leaving a pit. Osteoblasts then fill that pit with osteoid and mineralize it — weeks to dig out, months to fill back in. Removal and replacement are coupled, so osteoclasts never build anything; they only make room.

What decides where bone goes is mechanical load. Loaded bone thickens along its lines of stress; unloaded bone is resorbed within weeks, which is why a bedbound or casted patient, or an astronaut, loses density fast — and why weight-bearing exercise is the cheapest osteoporosis prevention there is.

The hormones that build and the hormones that thin

Growth and maintenance are hormonally driven, and the same hormone can do opposite things at different times.

Growth hormone, working through IGF-1 from the liver, drives cartilage proliferation at the plate; thyroid hormone is permissive, so untreated congenital hypothyroidism stunts growth. Sex steroids cause the pubertal spurt and then shut the plate — excess growth hormone before closure gives gigantism, after closure acromegaly.

Maintenance is the other half. Calcitriol, vitamin D activated by the kidney, lets the gut absorb the calcium mineralization needs. Estrogen restrains osteoclasts, which is why bone loss accelerates sharply after menopause. PTH held continuously high strips calcium from bone, while the same hormone in short daily pulses builds it. And glucocorticoids suppress osteoblasts directly — which is why months of prednisone quietly produce a fragile skeleton.

Two materials, two ways to break

Bone is a composite of two materials, and each fails differently.

The organic third is mostly collagen, which gives tensile strength and lets bone bend without snapping. The inorganic two-thirds is hydroxyapatite — calcium-phosphate crystals packed along those fibers for hardness and compressive strength. Collagen without mineral is rubbery; mineral without collagen is chalk.

That explains the diseases. Without vitamin D, osteoid is laid down but never properly mineralized — rickets in the growing child, whose soft bone bows, and osteomalacia in the adult, whose bone aches and cracks. In osteogenesis imperfecta the collagen is faulty, so bone is brittle and shatters. In osteoporosis both are lost together — what remains is normal bone, there is simply too little of it, which is why a hip gives way under an ordinary step.

How a fracture heals

A fracture heals in four overlapping stages, and each lays the scaffold for the next.

First a hematoma forms as torn vessels bleed into the break; the clot seals the gap while inflammatory cells clear debris, and the bone ends nearest the fracture die from lost blood supply. Next fibroblasts and chondroblasts build a fibrocartilaginous (soft) callus that splints the fragments within a week or two. Osteoblasts then convert it to a bony (hard) callus of spongy bone over the following weeks. Finally, months of remodeling replace that spongy bone with compact bone aligned to the loads the limb actually carries.

Every stage depends on that first bleed, which is why fractures in poorly perfused bone — the scaphoid, the femoral neck — are the ones you will see go to non-union.

Common misconceptions

  • "Osteoclasts build bone." Osteoblasts build; osteoclasts carve.
  • "PTH lowers blood calcium." PTH Pulls calcium into the blood. Calcitonin tones it down.
  • "Bone is inert." It remodels continuously and is the body's main calcium reservoir.
