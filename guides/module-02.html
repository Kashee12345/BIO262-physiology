High-yield facts

  • Diffusion moves solute; osmosis moves water. Neither costs ATP.
  • Water moves toward the higher solute concentration.
  • Glycolysis: cytoplasm, anaerobic, ~2 ATP. Aerobic respiration: mitochondria, ~30–32 ATP.
  • Transcription = nucleus (DNA→mRNA). Translation = ribosome (mRNA→protein).

Topic walkthrough — read this first

The membrane decides everything

A cell is defined by what it lets in. The plasma membrane is a phospholipid bilayer — water-loving heads out, water-fearing tails in — and it is selectively permeable: some things cross freely, some need help, some are refused.

Small nonpolar molecules like oxygen and carbon dioxide slip straight through. Charged ions and large polar molecules cannot, so the membrane studs itself with proteins: transport proteins move things, anchoring proteins hold the cytoskeleton, enzyme proteins catalyze, adhesion proteins join neighboring cells, and antigen proteins act as an identity badge saying this cell is one of ours.

Passive versus active — the only distinction that matters

Ask one question of any transport process: does it cost ATP?

If the answer is no, it is passive, and it must be moving down a gradient. Simple diffusion is solute drifting from crowded to empty. Facilitated diffusion is the same thing but through a protein, because the molecule cannot cross the lipid alone — it still costs nothing. Osmosis is the special case where water moves, and water always moves toward the higher solute concentration. The phrase to hold onto is: water follows salt.

If the answer is yes, it is active, and it can push against a gradient. The sodium–potassium pump throws three Na⁺ out and pulls two K⁺ in for every ATP. Because it exports more positive charge than it imports, it leaves the inside slightly negative — which is where the resting membrane potential comes from, and why this pump reappears in the nerve and muscle modules.

Organelles as jobs, not vocabulary

Learn each organelle as structure → function → what fails. The nucleus holds DNA and runs transcription. The nucleolus inside it builds ribosomes. Ribosomes translate mRNA into protein. Rough ER is studded with ribosomes and processes those proteins; smooth ER has none and instead makes lipids and detoxifies. The Golgi modifies and packages for shipping. Mitochondria make ATP. Lysosomes digest waste; peroxisomes handle fatty acids and free radicals.

The pattern predicts cell type. A cell secreting protein hormone will be crammed with rough ER and Golgi. A cell making steroids will be full of smooth ER. A muscle cell that never tires will be dense with mitochondria.

From gene to protein, and why mitosis matters

Transcription happens in the nucleus: DNA is copied into mRNA. Translation happens at the ribosome: mRNA is read three letters at a time and the matching amino acids are strung together. Three RNAs do the work — mRNA carries the message, tRNA ferries amino acids, and rRNA forms the ribosome itself and catalyzes the bonds.

Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells. That is the whole point: growth and repair need faithful copies. Contrast this with meiosis in Module 14, which deliberately produces variety.

What osmosis actually moves

Osmosis is the diffusion of water, and only water. The solute stays where it is, because the membrane is selectively permeable — it lets water through and holds the solute back. The water crosses instead, moving toward the side with more solute — the side where water is scarce. Say it to yourself as: water follows salt.

Tonicity describes what a solution does to a cell placed in it. In an isotonic solution water moves equally both ways and the cell keeps its shape. In a hypotonic solution water floods in and a red cell swells and lyses. In a hypertonic solution water leaves and the cell shrivels, or crenates. Which is why free water infused fast is dangerous — a cell cannot refuse water, and a swelling brain has nowhere to go.

Facilitated diffusion is still passive

The word facilitated makes you assume energy is being spent. It is not. Facilitated diffusion uses a membrane protein — either a channel or a carrier — but the molecule still travels down its concentration gradient, from high to low, and the cell pays nothing for the trip. The protein is there only because the molecule is too large or too charged to cross the oily interior of the bilayer alone. Glucose slips into most cells on a carrier; potassium leaks out through channels.

What separates it from simple diffusion is saturation: transporters are finite, so once they are all occupied, raising the concentration moves nothing extra. Any carrier can be saturated, which is why glucose spills into the urine once blood sugar climbs — the kidney's glucose carriers are already full.

The pump that pays for everything

The sodium-potassium pump is the largest energy bill most cells pay, and it earns it. For every ATP it spends, it pushes three sodium ions out and pulls two potassium ions in, both against their gradients — primary active transport, ATP spent directly on what is moved.

Two things follow. The pump keeps potassium high inside and sodium low, and potassium leaking back out down that gradient is the main reason the interior stays negative — the resting membrane potential nerve and muscle depend on; the uneven exchange adds a little more. And sodium, held low, strains to rush back in, so the cell hitches a passenger to that rush — secondary active transport, how glucose is reclaimed in gut and kidney, and why a failing pump starves the cell of glucose too.

When the molecule is too big to transport

Channels and pumps move one small molecule at a time. Anything larger — a bacterium, a droplet of fluid, a finished protein — has to be wrapped in membrane instead.

Endocytosis brings material in, and it takes three forms. Phagocytosis is cell eating: the membrane pushes out arms — pseudopods — around a solid particle, which is how neutrophils and macrophages clear bacteria. Pinocytosis is cell drinking, an indiscriminate sip of extracellular fluid. Receptor-mediated endocytosis is the selective one — a receptor must bind its specific ligand before the membrane pits inward. Exocytosis runs the process backwards, fusing a vesicle with the membrane to release hormones, neurotransmitters or enzymes outward. Which is why a defective LDL receptor leaves cholesterol circulating — the cell cannot import what it cannot grip.

The cell cycle and its checkpoints

Most of a cell's life is not mitosis. Interphase occupies the great majority of it and has three parts: G1, where the cell grows and does its actual job; S phase, where the DNA is copied so each daughter cell will receive a complete set; and G2, where the copy is proofread and the machinery of division is built.

Mitosis then divides the nucleus in four stages — prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase — and cytokinesis pinches the cytoplasm into two cells. At fixed points in the cycle sit checkpoints that halt everything if DNA is damaged or chromosomes are misaligned, and cells beyond repair are told to self-destruct by apoptosis. Which is why cancer is at heart a checkpoint failure — damaged cells that should have stopped keep dividing.

Common misconceptions

  • "Osmosis moves solute." Osmosis moves water. Diffusion moves solute.
  • "Facilitated diffusion uses ATP." It uses a protein but no ATP. Only active transport costs energy.
  • "Water moves toward low solute." Backwards. Water follows solute, moving toward the more concentrated side.
