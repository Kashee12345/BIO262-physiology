High-yield facts

  • Innate = fast, non-specific, no memory. Adaptive = slower, specific, has memory.
  • NK cells are lymphocytes but act innately.
  • Plasma cells (activated B cells) make the antibodies; helper T cells coordinate everything.
  • The lymphatic system returns leaked interstitial fluid to the blood — blockage causes edema.

Topic walkthrough — read this first

Two immune systems, working together

Innate immunity is what you are born with: fast, non-specific, no memory. Skin and mucous membranes, neutrophils and macrophages, NK cells, complement, inflammation and fever. It responds in minutes but treats every intruder the same.

Adaptive immunity is specific and remembers. It takes days on first exposure, but the second time it is dramatically faster — which is the entire principle behind vaccination.

NK cells are the confusing case: they are lymphocytes, which feels adaptive, but they attack without prior exposure, so they are innate.

Who does what in the adaptive response

Helper T cells (CD4) coordinate. They activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells and release cytokines — which is precisely why HIV, by destroying them, disables both arms at once.

B cells recognize antigen and become plasma cells, which mass-produce antibody. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8) kill infected cells directly. Memory cells of both kinds persist afterwards, and are the reason the second exposure is so much easier.

The lymphatic system does two jobs

First, immunity: lymph nodes filter lymph and hold lymphocytes and macrophages, the spleen filters blood and clears old red cells, the thymus matures T cells, and MALT guards mucosal surfaces.

Second, and easy to forget: fluid balance. Capillaries leak fluid into tissue continuously. The lymphatics collect that excess and return it to the bloodstream at the subclavian veins. Block the drainage and the fluid has nowhere to go — that is lymphedema.

Antibodies, and which one is where

All five immunoglobulins do the same basic thing — bind antigen — but each is deployed differently.

IgM is produced first in a new infection, so finding it suggests a current or recent one. IgG is the most abundant, arrives later, and is the only one that crosses the placenta — which is how a newborn borrows its mother's immunity for a few months. IgA guards mucosal surfaces and is in breast milk. IgE binds mast cells and drives allergy and parasite defense. IgD sits on B cells as a receptor.

If a question mentions a newborn, think IgG. Mucosa or milk, think IgA. Allergy, think IgE.

Active and passive immunity

Active immunity means you made the response yourself — through infection or vaccination. It is slow to establish but leaves memory, so it lasts.

Passive immunity means someone else's antibodies were handed to you — across the placenta, in breast milk, or as an injection. Protection is immediate, which is exactly why it is used in emergencies, but it fades within weeks to months and leaves no memory, because your own B cells were never involved.

Immediate but temporary, or slow but lasting. That trade-off is the whole distinction.

The complement system, all three pathways

Complement is a set of plasma proteins that circulate inactive until one of three pathways switches them on. The classical pathway starts when C1 binds an antibody already stuck to an antigen. The lectin pathway starts when mannose-binding lectin recognizes sugars on microbes but not on yours. The alternative pathway starts by itself, when activated C3 lands on a foreign surface lacking your regulatory proteins.

All three converge on one cascade with three outcomes. C3b coats the microbe so phagocytes can grip it — opsonization. C3a and C5a drive inflammation, and C5a especially pulls neutrophils in. C5b through C9 assemble the membrane attack complex, a pore that lyses the cell. Which is why people missing late complement proteins get repeated Neisseria infections — antibody alone cannot drill that hole.

The innate arsenal, layer by layer

Innate defense is layered, and a microbe meets those layers in order. The barriers come first — intact skin, mucus and cilia sweeping the airway, stomach acid, lysozyme in tears and saliva, and normal flora holding the space.

Break through those and cells respond. Neutrophils arrive first and phagocytose. Macrophages engulf and present what they find. Dendritic cells carry antigen to the lymph nodes, where adaptive immunity begins. Eosinophils attack parasites, while basophils and mast cells release histamine. Natural killer cells are lymphocytes, but innate ones — they kill any cell that has stopped displaying normal self markers, without ever having met it before. Interferons, complement, inflammation and fever run alongside. None of it requires prior exposure, which is why it works in minutes rather than days.

MHC, and why CD4 and CD8 see different things

A T cell cannot see free antigen — only fragments displayed on a major histocompatibility complex molecule — and the two classes explain the division of labor. MHC class I sits on every nucleated cell, displaying proteins made inside it. If a virus replicates there, its fragments appear on the surface, and CD8 cytotoxic T cells read class I and kill the cell outright. MHC class II sits only on antigen-presenting cells — dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells — displaying material swallowed from outside. CD4 helper T cells read class II and release cytokines that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells and macrophages.

Which is why HIV is so devastating: it destroys CD4 cells, and without the coordinator, otherwise healthy B cells and CD8 cells never get switched on.

Why the second exposure is different

The first time you meet an antigen the response is slow — several days while the few lymphocytes that happen to fit divide into a clone. Antibody appears late, at a low level, and it is mostly IgM. That lag is why a first infection makes you ill.

Some of that clone becomes memory B and T cells, which persist for years. On re-exposure the secondary response starts within hours, climbs far higher, lasts longer and is mostly IgG. You often clear the organism before symptoms begin. That is exactly what a vaccine buys, and why boosters exist — each repeat exposure enlarges the memory pool. It is also why a laboratory finding of IgM says recent infection while IgG says past infection or vaccination.

When tolerance fails

Lymphocytes are built with random receptors, so some inevitably recognize you. Tolerance is the screening that removes them. T cells are tested in the thymus: those that cannot read your MHC die, and those that bind your own proteins too strongly are deleted. B cells are screened separately in bone marrow, where those binding your tissue are deleted or made to rebuild their receptor.

When that screening fails you get autoimmunity — Hashimoto thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, lupus. None of these is weakness. The system is working at full strength and simply aiming at the wrong target, which is why the treatment is immunosuppression rather than immune stimulation — the opposite of what you give in immunodeficiency, where the response is too small rather than misdirected.

Common misconceptions

  • "NK cells are adaptive." They are lymphocytes but act without prior exposure — innate.
  • "Passive immunity gives lasting protection." It fades in weeks to months and leaves no memory.
  • "Autoimmunity means a weak immune system." It is misdirected, not weak.
