High-yield facts

  • Inhalation: diaphragm contracts → thoracic volume ↑ → pressure ↓ → air in (Boyle's law).
  • Surfactant (Type II cells) lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse.
  • O₂ ~98% on hemoglobin; CO₂ ~70% as bicarbonate.
  • Retained CO₂ → respiratory acidosis; blown off CO₂ → respiratory alkalosis.

Topic walkthrough — read this first

Breathing is a pressure problem

Air moves down a pressure gradient, and your diaphragm creates that gradient. On inhalation the diaphragm contracts and flattens, the thoracic cavity gets bigger, and by Boyle's law a bigger space means lower pressure — so pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric and air flows in. On quiet exhalation the diaphragm relaxes, the cavity shrinks, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out. Quiet exhalation is passive; it costs nothing but elastic recoil.

Surfactant, made by Type II alveolar cells, lowers surface tension so alveoli do not collapse on themselves. A premature infant without it has to fight to reinflate the lungs with every breath.

Ventilation, perfusion, diffusion

Three separate requirements, and all three must work. Ventilation is air reaching the alveoli. Perfusion is blood reaching the capillaries around them. Diffusion is gas actually crossing the membrane, driven by the partial pressure gradient.

A pulmonary embolism ventilates fine but does not perfuse. Pulmonary edema perfuses fine but the membrane is too thick to diffuse across. Naming which of the three has failed is usually the whole question.

How the gases travel

Oxygen is barely soluble, so about 98% rides on hemoglobin and only a couple of percent dissolves. Carbon dioxide is different: roughly 70% travels as bicarbonate in the plasma, about 23% on hemoglobin, and the rest dissolved.

That bicarbonate route is why respiration and pH are inseparable. CO₂ plus water makes carbonic acid, which releases H⁺. Retain CO₂ and you become acidotic; blow it off and you become alkalotic. The lungs are a pH organ as much as a gas organ.

Lung volumes, and why some air never leaves

A normal quiet breath is tidal volume, roughly 500 mL. You can inhale a lot more on top of it and exhale a lot more beyond it, and together with tidal volume that maximum sweep is vital capacity.

But no matter how hard you blow out, some air stays — residual volume. That is not a flaw. It keeps alveoli open between breaths so they do not have to be reinflated from collapsed each time, and it means gas exchange continues even while you are exhaling.

Compliance: how easily lungs inflate

Compliance is how readily the lungs expand for a given effort. Surfactant raises it by cutting surface tension. Scarring lowers it — in pulmonary fibrosis the lungs are stiff, and every breath costs more work.

Compliance and airway obstruction are different problems and it is worth keeping them apart. Fibrosis makes lungs hard to inflate. COPD makes air hard to get out, because narrowed airways and lost elastic recoil trap it on exhalation. Restrictive versus obstructive, in one sentence each.

Which muscles do the work of breathing

Quiet inspiration is the only part of normal breathing that costs muscular energy. The diaphragm flattens and the external intercostals lift the ribs outward, the thorax enlarges, and air follows the pressure drop inward. The diaphragm alone does about 70 percent of that work.

Quiet expiration is passive. You simply stop contracting and the stretched elastic tissue of the lungs and chest wall recoils — no muscle needed. Only forced expiration recruits the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles, and only forced inspiration calls in the scalenes, sternocleidomastoid and pectoralis minor.

Which is why a patient using neck muscles to pull air in, or belly muscles to push it out, is telling you the effortless version of breathing has already failed.

The three cells lining an alveolus

An alveolus has three cell types, and only one of them exchanges gas.

Type I alveolar cells are flat squamous cells covering about 95 percent of the surface. Being thin is the entire point — oxygen and carbon dioxide cross them almost instantly. Type II cells are cuboidal, occupy the rest, and secrete surfactant, the detergent-like film that lowers surface tension so an alveolus does not collapse on itself. They also serve as the repair stem cell after lung injury. Alveolar macrophages, or dust cells, patrol the surface and engulf whatever was inhaled past the airways.

Surfactant does not reach adequate levels until roughly week 34 of gestation, which is why a premature infant's alveoli collapse at every breath out and must be reopened from nothing — neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

How hemoglobin decides when to let go

Each hemoglobin molecule carries four oxygen molecules, and binding the first makes the next easier — cooperative binding. That is why the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve is S-shaped: in the lungs saturation holds near 98 percent even as the partial pressure dips, while in the tissues the curve falls steeply, so a small pressure drop unloads a lot of oxygen.

Four things shift the curve right, so hemoglobin releases oxygen more readily: rising carbon dioxide, falling pH, rising temperature and rising 2,3-BPG. The carbon dioxide and pH half of that is the Bohr effect. The opposite conditions shift it left and hold oxygen on.

Which is why working muscle — hot, acidic and loaded with carbon dioxide — strips far more oxygen from the same arterial blood than resting tissue does.

Carbon dioxide travels as bicarbonate

Oxygen and carbon dioxide do not travel the same way. About 70 percent of carbon dioxide is carried as bicarbonate, 20 percent rides on the globin part of hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin, and only 7 to 10 percent dissolves in plasma.

That bicarbonate is made inside the red cell: carbonic anhydrase joins carbon dioxide to water, forming carbonic acid, which splits into a hydrogen ion and bicarbonate. The bicarbonate leaves for the plasma and a chloride ion moves in to balance the charge — the chloride shift. In the lung the reaction reverses and the gas is blown off.

Because that reaction makes acid, ventilation sets pH — hypoventilating traps carbon dioxide and drops the pH, while hyperventilating blows it off, which is why a panicking patient who over-breathes goes alkalotic and tingles.

When ventilation and perfusion do not match

Air and blood must arrive at the same alveolus for exchange to happen, and the ventilation to perfusion ratio is about 0.8. There are two ways to break it.

A shunt is perfusion without ventilation — blood flows past an alveolus with no air, as in pneumonia, atelectasis or pulmonary edema. That blood reaches the left heart still deoxygenated, and extra oxygen helps little because air cannot reach it. Dead space is the mirror image: ventilation without perfusion, classically a pulmonary embolism blocking flow to an alveolus that is perfectly well ventilated.

The lung defends itself with hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction: vessels serving poorly ventilated regions narrow, pushing blood toward alveoli that actually have air. Which is why a small mismatch stays invisible while a large one drops saturation fast.

Common misconceptions

  • "Exhalation is active." Quiet exhalation is passive elastic recoil.
  • "CO₂ travels mostly on hemoglobin." ~70% travels as bicarbonate in plasma.
  • "Type II cells perform gas exchange." Type I exchange gas; Type II make surfactant.
