High-yield facts

  • S1 = AV valves closing (start of systole). S2 = semilunar valves closing (start of diastole).
  • CO = HR × stroke volume (~5 L/min at rest).
  • Conduction: SA node → AV node → Bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibers.
  • P = atrial depolarization, QRS = ventricular depolarization, T = ventricular repolarization.

Topic walkthrough — read this first

The heart as a pump

Four chambers, two circuits. The right side receives deoxygenated blood from the body and sends it to the lungs — the pulmonary circuit. The left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pushes it to the body — the systemic circuit. The left ventricle has by far the thickest wall because it has by far the longest push.

Valves make flow one-way. The AV valves (tricuspid right, mitral left) open during diastole to let the ventricles fill and slam shut when the ventricles contract — that closure is S1, the "lub". The semilunar valves (pulmonary and aortic) open during ejection and snap shut as the ventricles relax — that is S2, the "dub". The two heart sounds are simply two sets of valves closing.

Cardiac output, and the two ways to change it

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, around 5 liters a minute at rest. Anything that changes output must change one of those two numbers.

Heart rate is set by autonomic tone — sympathetic speeds it, vagal parasympathetic slows it. Stroke volume depends on preload (how full the ventricle got, which depends on venous return), contractility (how hard it squeezes), and afterload (the pressure it must push against). Stretch a ventricle more and it contracts harder — the Frank–Starling relationship — which is how the heart automatically matches output to return.

The heart as an electrical system

The SA node depolarizes spontaneously and fastest, so it sets the rhythm. The impulse spreads across the atria, then reaches the AV node, which deliberately delays it — that pause is what lets the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract. From there it runs down the Bundle of His, splits into bundle branches, and spreads through the Purkinje fibers so the ventricles contract as one coordinated unit from the apex upward.

On an ECG: P is atrial depolarization, QRS is ventricular depolarization, and T is ventricular repolarization. Atrial repolarization does happen — it is simply buried inside the much larger QRS.

Preload, afterload, contractility

Three words govern stroke volume, and they are easy to keep straight if you picture the ventricle.

Preload is how full it got before it squeezed — it depends on venous return. More filling stretches the fibers, and a stretched ventricle contracts harder. That is the Frank–Starling relationship, and it is how the heart matches output to whatever comes back without needing instructions.

Contractility is how hard it squeezes at a given stretch — sympathetic stimulation raises it. Afterload is the pressure it must overcome to open the aortic valve and eject. Raise afterload, as chronic hypertension does, and the ventricle must work harder for every beat — which is why untreated hypertension eventually thickens and then fails the left ventricle.

Why the heart cannot cramp

Skeletal muscle can be stimulated repeatedly until the twitches fuse into one sustained contraction — tetanus. If the heart could do that, it would seize and never refill, and you would die.

It cannot, because cardiac muscle has an unusually long refractory period. The plateau phase — sustained by calcium entering while potassium leaves — keeps the cell unresponsive until the contraction is over and relaxation has begun. The heart is physically incapable of a second beat before the first has finished. That constraint is a safety feature.

The two heart sounds, and which valves make them

Put a stethoscope on a chest and you hear lub-dub: two sounds, four valves.

The first sound, S1, is the atrioventricular valves slamming shut — the tricuspid on the right, the mitral on the left. They close the moment ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, so S1 marks the start of systole. The second sound, S2, is the semilunar valves — pulmonary and aortic — snapping shut as the ventricles relax and arterial pressure pushes blood back toward them, marking the start of diastole.

No valve is pulled open or pushed shut by muscle — every one moves on a pressure difference alone, and the papillary muscles only anchor the AV valves so they cannot turn inside out. Which is why a stiff or leaking valve gives the turbulent flow you hear as a murmur.

Reading the ECG wave by wave

An ECG records electrical events, not contractions — each wave is a voltage change that a mechanical event follows.

The P wave is atrial depolarization. The flat PR segment after it is the signal being held at the AV node, giving the atria time to finish emptying. The QRS complex is ventricular depolarization — large, because the ventricles carry far more muscle. Atrial repolarization happens at the same moment, buried inside the QRS, which is why you never see a wave for it. The ST segment should sit flat during contraction, and the T wave is ventricular repolarization.

That flat segment matters clinically: starved of oxygen, myocardium shifts it — depressed in ischemia, elevated when muscle is injuring and infarcting — which is how a blocked coronary announces itself on the monitor.

Why the heart beats itself

A heart removed from the body keeps beating, and a transplanted heart beats before any nerve reaches it. That is because pacemaker cells have no stable resting potential — sodium leaks inward through funny channels until the cell drifts to threshold and fires on its own. That drift is the heartbeat.

Every part of the conduction system can do this, at different speeds. The SA node drifts fastest — around 100 a minute on its own, 60 to 100 with vagal braking — so it sets the rhythm; the AV node manages 40 to 60, and the bundle of His, bundle branches and Purkinje fibers only 20 to 40.

The fastest pacemaker wins. Which is why, if the SA node fails, a slower site takes over — the patient goes bradycardic, but stays alive.

Why your resting pulse is not 100

If the SA node fires at around 100 on its own, why is your resting pulse nearer 70? Because the vagus nerve brakes it continuously. That standing parasympathetic traffic — vagal tone — releases acetylcholine onto the node, slows the drift to threshold and holds you below your own intrinsic rate.

Sympathetic fibers release norepinephrine, and the adrenal medulla adds circulating epinephrine — both doing the opposite, through three effects: chronotropy (rate at the SA node), dromotropy (conduction speed at the AV node) and inotropy (force of contraction). Parasympathetic supply reaches the nodes but barely touches ventricular muscle, so the vagus changes rate far more than force.

When you stand and your pulse jumps, the first thing that happened was vagal withdrawal — releasing the brake is quicker than pressing the accelerator.

The heart feeds itself during diastole

The heart sits in a chamber full of blood and cannot use a drop of it. Myocardium is fed by the coronary arteries, which branch off the aorta just above the aortic valve — the first vessels off the pump.

Here is the catch. During systole the contracting left ventricle squeezes its own vessels almost shut, so the coronaries fill during diastole, when the muscle is relaxed and the closed aortic valve holds a column of pressurized blood above them. Left-heart perfusion is a diastolic event.

Now think about tachycardia. As rate climbs, diastole shortens far more than systole — a racing heart demands more oxygen while giving itself less time to collect any. Which is why chest pain appears on exertion, and why slowing the rate is so often the treatment.

Common misconceptions

  • "S1 is the semilunar valves." S1 = AV valves closing. S2 = semilunar valves closing.
  • "Cardiac output is just heart rate." CO = HR × stroke volume. Both matter.
  • "The QRS shows atrial repolarization." It shows ventricular depolarization; atrial repolarization is hidden inside it.
