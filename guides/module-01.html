Topic walkthrough — read this first

What physiology actually asks

Anatomy asks what is this and where is it. Physiology asks what does it do and how. Every question in this course is really the same question in different clothing: here is a structure, here is its job, and here is what goes wrong when it fails.

You will see three scales. Cell physiology looks inside a single cell — how it makes ATP, how it moves glucose across its membrane. Organ physiology zooms out to one organ, like how the kidney filters blood. Systemic physiology looks at organs cooperating, like the heart and lungs together during exercise. Pathophysiology is what this becomes when something breaks.

Bonds, and why water behaves the way it does

Atoms bond to fill their outer shell, and there are only really two ways to do it. In an ionic bond one atom hands an electron over completely — sodium gives its lone outer electron to chlorine, both end up charged, and opposite charges cling together. In a covalent bond they share instead. If they share evenly it is nonpolar; if one atom pulls harder it is polar, and the molecule ends up with a slightly negative end and a slightly positive end.

Water is the polar molecule that matters. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so oxygen sits slightly negative and the hydrogens slightly positive. That lopsidedness is why water dissolves salts — it surrounds ions and pulls them apart — and why water molecules stick to each other through hydrogen bonds. Hydrogen bonds are weak, and that weakness is the point: they form and break easily, which is exactly what DNA needs to unzip and what proteins need to change shape.

Homeostasis is a thermostat, not a freeze-frame

Homeostasis does not mean values stay still. It means they wobble around a set point and get pulled back. Every loop has the same five parts: a stimulus knocks a value off target, a receptor notices, a control center decides, an effector acts, and the response feeds back.

In negative feedback the response opposes the stimulus and therefore switches itself off. You get too warm, you sweat, you cool down, you stop sweating. Nearly everything in your body works this way.

In positive feedback the response amplifies the stimulus, so it escalates until something external stops it. Labor is the classic case — contractions release oxytocin, oxytocin strengthens contractions, and it only ends with delivery. Clotting is the other. Positive feedback is not "bad"; it is just the rarer design, used when the body needs to finish something decisively.

pH, and why 7.35–7.45 is so tight

pH measures hydrogen ions, and it runs backwards: more H⁺ means a lower number. It is also logarithmic, so each unit is a tenfold change — pH 2 has ten million times more H⁺ than pH 9.

Blood sits between 7.35 and 7.45. That window is narrow because protein shape depends on pH, and enzyme function depends on protein shape. Drift outside it and a great many reactions degrade at once. Below 7.35 is acidosis; above 7.45 is alkalosis.

Buffers are what hold the line. A buffer soaks up H⁺ when acid rises and releases it when acid falls — it works in both directions. The main one in blood is the bicarbonate system, which is also why breathing (CO₂) and the kidneys (bicarbonate) both end up controlling your pH.

Electrons, shells, and why ions matter

An atom is a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons in shells, and only the outermost — the valence shell — does chemistry. An atom is stable when that shell is full and reactive when it is not, so it gives away, takes, or shares electrons until it is full. Lose an electron and the atom becomes a positive cation; gain one and it becomes a negative anion.

Your electrolyte panel is that rule applied. Sodium holds water in the extracellular fluid, potassium sets the resting membrane potential, calcium triggers contraction and clotting, magnesium activates enzymes, chloride follows sodium, and bicarbonate and phosphate buffer acid. Charge is what makes each of these do work — which is why a shift in potassium too small to notice can stop a heart outright.

The buffers, and the three lines of defense

A buffer is a pair of molecules that can absorb a hydrogen ion or release one, so pH barely moves when acid arrives. You run three of them. The bicarbonate system handles extracellular fluid and does most of the work; the phosphate system works inside cells and in the kidney tubules. Protein buffers, hemoglobin above all, work wherever protein is, taking up hydrogen and handing it back.

Buffers act in seconds, but they only bind the acid, they do not remove it. The lungs follow within minutes, blowing off carbon dioxide before it becomes carbonic acid. The kidneys take hours to days, but they alone excrete fixed acid and rebuild the bicarbonate you spent — which is why acid-base trouble in kidney failure deepens for days instead of correcting itself.

Positive feedback is not the villain

Negative feedback reverses a change, and it holds nearly every variable steady. Positive feedback amplifies a change instead, driving the variable further from its start, which students read as pathology. It is not. It is what the body uses when a job needs finishing, not balancing.

There are only a handful. In labor, cervical stretch drives oxytocin, which drives stronger contractions, which stretch the cervix further. Activated platelets recruit more platelets in clotting, sodium entry opens more sodium channels in the action potential, rising estrogen tips the pituitary into the LH surge, and suckling drives the milk ejection reflex. Each loop ends because something outside it removes the stimulus — the baby is delivered, the vessel is sealed — which is why positive feedback is dangerous only when that end never arrives.

Set points can move

A thermostat has one setting. You have several, and they move.

In fever, pyrogens raise the hypothalamic set point to thirty-nine degrees Celsius; your temperature has not escaped control, it is being controlled to a higher target — which is why you shiver under blankets while already hot. At altitude, erythropoietin resets red cell mass upward over weeks. In chronic hypertension, baroreceptors reset and treat the raised pressure as normal. Even across a day, temperature, cortisol and blood pressure follow a circadian swing.

Homeostasis does not mean a value never changes; it means a value is defended. When a number sits outside the range, ask whether control has failed or the target has moved — cooling a fever fights the body's own target, while cooling heat stroke saves a life.

Gradients are stored energy

Ions do not drift at random. The body spends roughly a fifth of its resting energy running the sodium-potassium pump, which pushes three sodium out for every two potassium in, against both concentrations. That looks wasteful until you see what it buys.

The result is a gradient — sodium high outside, potassium high inside — and a gradient is stored energy, like water held behind a dam. Opening a sodium channel releases it, and that release is the action potential and the muscle twitch; the same gradient drags glucose into the gut lining alongside sodium. Nothing is built on demand; it is already charged and waiting.

Which is why cells swell within minutes of losing blood flow and die soon after — no oxygen, no ATP, no pump, and the dam drains.

Common misconceptions

  • "Higher pH means more hydrogen ions." Inverted. pH is inversely related to H⁺ — pH 2 has ten million times more H⁺ than pH 9.
  • "Positive feedback is harmful." No. Labor and clotting both depend on it. It amplifies rather than opposes; that is the only difference.
  • "Homeostasis means values never change." Values oscillate around a set point. Homeostasis is dynamic, not static.
