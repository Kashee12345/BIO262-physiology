High-yield facts

  • Near vision = lens rounds up. Far vision = lens flattens.
  • Rods = dim light, no color, periphery. Cones = color and detail, fovea.
  • Semicircular canals = rotation. Utricle/saccule + otoliths = gravity and linear acceleration.
  • 20/200 is far worse than 20/20; 20/15 is better than average.

Topic walkthrough — read this first

How the eye focuses

The cornea does most of the bending of light; the lens does the fine adjustment. For a near object the ciliary muscle contracts and the lens rounds up, bending light more. For a distant object it flattens. This is accommodation, and it is the reverse of what most students first guess.

As the lens stiffens with age it can no longer round up, which is presbyopia — the reason reading glasses arrive in middle age.

Turning light into a signal

The retina has two photoreceptors with opposite specialities. Rods are extremely light-sensitive but give no color and poor detail, and sit in the periphery — they are your night vision. Cones need bright light but give color and sharp detail, and cluster at the fovea, which is why you turn your eyes toward whatever you want to see clearly.

Light strikes opsin, which changes shape and starts a cascade. The signal passes photoreceptor → bipolar cell → ganglion cell, and ganglion cell axons leave the eye as the optic nerve. Where they exit there are no photoreceptors at all — your blind spot.

Hearing is a chain of energy conversions

Sound starts as pressure waves in air. The pinna collects them. The tympanic membrane converts them into mechanical vibration. The three ossicles amplify that vibration and drive the oval window. That moves fluid inside the cochlea, which bends hair cells, and there the mechanical becomes electrical. Cranial nerve VIII carries it to the temporal lobe.

Break the chain early — wax, a perforated drum, stiff ossicles — and you get conductive loss. Break it at the hair cells or nerve and you get sensorineural loss.

Balance: two different questions

The inner ear answers two separate questions with two separate structures. Am I tilted, or accelerating in a straight line? is answered by the utricle and saccule, where calcium crystals called otoliths sit on a gel and drag on hair cells when gravity or acceleration pulls them.

Am I rotating? is answered by the three semicircular canals, set at right angles to each other. When your head turns, the fluid inside lags behind and bends hair cells in the ampulla. That lag is also why you still feel like you are spinning for a few seconds after you stop.

Where the image goes after the retina

Light becomes a signal in the retina, but you do not see anything until the occipital lobe assembles it. Axons of the ganglion cells leave the eye as the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm only the fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross, while the temporal fibers stay on their own side, so each optic tract carries the opposite half of the visual world rather than the opposite eye.

The signal then relays in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and travels through the optic radiations to the primary visual cortex. Damage before the chiasm blinds one eye; damage at the chiasm — classically a pituitary tumor — removes both outer fields, which is why such a patient clips door frames on both sides.

Color, detail, and seeing in the dark

Where photoreceptors sit matters as much as how they work. Cones come in three kinds, tuned to short, medium and long wavelengths, and your brain reads color from the ratio of their firing, which is why losing one type distorts color rather than removing it. Cones crowd the fovea centralis, each wired almost one to one, giving fine detail but only in good light.

Rods pack the periphery and funnel many receptors onto one pathway, summing faint light into a usable signal at the cost of detail. Their rhodopsin bleaches the moment light hits it and takes twenty to thirty minutes to rebuild — which is why a dark room looks black when you first walk in, and why a faint star is easier to see just off to one side.

When the image lands in the wrong place

A normal eye focuses light on the retina itself. In myopia the eyeball is too long, so the image forms in front of the retina and distance blurs — a diverging concave lens pushes it back. In hyperopia the eyeball is too short, so the image forms behind the retina and near work blurs — a converging convex lens pulls it forward. Astigmatism is uneven corneal curvature, so nothing focuses sharply at any distance.

Presbyopia is different: the ageing lens stiffens and cannot round up for near vision, which is why reading glasses arrive in your forties. Acuity compares you with a normal eye: 20/200 means you read at twenty feet what a normal eye reads at two hundred, so a bigger bottom number is worse vision, not better.

How the cochlea codes pitch and loudness

Pitch and volume are carried by two different features of the same wave. The basilar membrane is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at the apex, so high frequencies peak near the base and low frequencies near the apex. Whichever region vibrates most bends the stereocilia of its hair cells against the tectorial membrane, and the temporal lobe reads pitch from which fibers of cranial nerve VIII fired. Loudness is those cells bending further and firing faster.

That anatomy also sorts hearing loss. Conductive loss blocks the chain before the receptors — wax, middle ear fluid, a fixed ossicle — so clearing it restores hearing. Sensorineural loss is dead hair cells or nerve, from noise, ageing or ototoxic drugs, and because hair cells never regrow it is permanent.

What the vestibular receptors can and cannot feel

Every vestibular receptor is a hair cell; what sits on top decides what it detects. In the three semicircular canals — one per plane of space — hair cells of the crista ampullaris project into a gel cupula, and only moving fluid bends it. When your head starts or stops rotating the endolymph lags behind and deflects the cupula, which makes these canals detectors of angular acceleration and nothing else.

Gravity and linear acceleration are the job of the utricle and saccule, whose maculae are capped by otoliths — crystals heavy enough to sag when you tilt or pull away in a car. Stop spinning and the endolymph keeps moving, so your canals still report turning while your eyes report stillness — which is why the room seems to spin.

Common misconceptions

  • "The lens flattens for near vision." Opposite. It rounds up for near, flattens for far.
  • "20/200 is better than 20/20." Far worse — the larger the second number, the poorer the acuity.
  • "The semicircular canals detect gravity." They detect rotation. The utricle and saccule detect gravity and linear acceleration.
