  • Descending limb = water out. Ascending limb = ions out, impermeable to water.

Topic walkthrough — read this first

What kidneys are actually doing

Filtering is only the first job. The kidneys also regulate blood volume and pressure, balance electrolytes, control acid–base status by excreting H⁺ and reclaiming bicarbonate, excrete urea and creatinine, and secrete two hormones — erythropoietin to drive red cell production and renin to raise blood pressure.

One sentence explains most of it: water follows sodium. Retain sodium and water comes with it, so blood volume and pressure rise. Excrete sodium and water leaves too. That is how the kidney controls pressure, and how diuretics work.

Walking the nephron

Blood arrives through the afferent arteriole and enters the glomerulus, where high pressure forces water, ions, glucose and small wastes into Bowman's capsule. Proteins and blood cells are too large to pass — finding protein in urine means the filter is damaged. The efferent arteriole leaving is narrower than the one arriving, and that constriction is what sustains the pressure filtration needs.

The proximal convoluted tubule does most of the reabsorption, reclaiming essentially all glucose and amino acids. Then the loop of Henle does something clever: the descending limb lets water out but not ions, while the ascending limb pumps ions out but is impermeable to water. Removing salt without water builds a salty gradient in the medulla — and that gradient is what later lets the collecting duct pull water back and produce concentrated urine.

The distal convoluted tubule fine-tunes under aldosterone, and the collecting duct makes the final call on concentration under ADH.

Reabsorption and secretion point opposite ways

Reabsorption moves things filtrate → blood: keeping what is useful. Secretion moves things blood → filtrate: dumping extra H⁺, K⁺ and drugs. Filtration is indiscriminate; these two are where the precision happens. Mixing up the direction is the single most common error on this material.

Making gametes: two very different processes

Spermatogenesis is continuous from puberty. One primary spermatocyte goes through both meiotic divisions and yields four sperm, which then mature in the epididymis where they gain motility.

Oogenesis is almost the opposite. Every primary oocyte is formed before birth and then arrests. Each cycle after puberty, one completes the first division and is ovulated — but what is released is a secondary oocyte, arrested again in meiosis II. It only finishes, and only truly becomes an ovum, if a sperm penetrates it. One precursor yields one ovum plus discarded polar bodies.

The cycle, and what ends it

Days 1–5 are menstrual: the lining sheds, and it sheds because progesterone fell, not because anything rose. Days 1–13 are follicular: FSH matures follicles and the dominant one pours out estrogen, which rebuilds the endometrium. Around day 14 that estrogen peak flips to positive feedback and triggers an LH surge, which ruptures the follicle — ovulation. Days 15–28 are luteal: the leftover follicle becomes the corpus luteum and secretes progesterone to hold the lining ready. No pregnancy, corpus luteum dies, progesterone falls, and the cycle restarts.

Menopause is when the ovaries stop responding. Estrogen and progesterone fall — but FSH and LH rise, because the pituitary has lost its negative feedback and keeps shouting at ovaries that no longer answer. That counterintuitive pair is examined constantly.

Filtration is a pressure balance

Filtration happens because blood in the glomerulus sits under more pressure than the fluid in the capsule around it. Three pressures settle the argument: glomerular hydrostatic pressure (about 55 mmHg) pushes fluid out, capsular hydrostatic pressure (about 15) pushes back, and blood colloid osmotic pressure from plasma proteins (about 30) pulls it in. That leaves roughly 10 mmHg outward and a GFR near 125 mL/min.

The filtration membrane — fenestrated endothelium, basement membrane, podocyte slits — passes water and small solutes but holds back cells and proteins. The kidney defends that pressure, constricting the afferent arteriole when systemic pressure rises and the efferent when it falls — which is why a patient in shock stops making urine, and why protein in the urine tells you the membrane itself has been damaged.

Why the medulla stays salty

Concentrated urine is only possible because the kidney keeps its deep tissue salty, and the loop of Henle builds that gradient. The descending limb passes water but not salt, so filtrate loses water and grows concentrated. The ascending limb does the opposite — it pumps sodium and chloride out while staying waterproof, so the fluid leaving is dilute and the surrounding medulla turns increasingly salty. Urea recycling from the deep collecting duct deepens it, and the hairpin vasa recta pick that salt up going down and hand it back coming up, rather than washing the gradient out.

That gradient is what ADH exploits: it inserts aquaporins into the collecting duct and water follows the gradient out — which is why someone who cannot make ADH passes liters of dilute urine.

Storing urine, and letting it go

Once fluid leaves the collecting duct it is urine, and nothing downstream changes its composition. The ureters carry it down by peristalsis and enter the bladder at an oblique angle, so a filling bladder pinches them shut and urine cannot reflux toward the kidney. The bladder wall, the detrusor muscle, stretches to hold roughly 400-600 mL. Two sphincters guard the exit: the internal urethral sphincter is smooth muscle and involuntary, the external is skeletal and under your control.

At about 200-300 mL, stretch receptors fire and a parasympathetic reflex from S2-S4 contracts the detrusor and relaxes the internal sphincter. Voiding still waits on the external sphincter — which is why a spinal injury above the sacrum leaves a bladder that, once spinal shock passes, empties on reflex alone, without warning.

What actually gets ovulated

Follicles are not made on demand — you are born with every one you will ever have, each holding a primary oocyte frozen in prophase I since before birth. A primordial follicle becomes a primary follicle, then a secondary follicle as the granulosa layers thicken, then an antral follicle once a fluid-filled cavity opens, and finally one mature (Graafian) follicle outgrows the rest.

The LH surge finishes meiosis I, so what leaves the ovary is a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II with a polar body beside it — not an ovum. It becomes an ovum only if a sperm penetrates it. The emptied follicle becomes the corpus luteum and then scars into a corpus albicans — unless an embryo's hCG rescues it, which is exactly what a pregnancy test detects.

Sperm are made in one place, finished in another

Sperm production runs continuously in the seminiferous tubules, and two cell types make it possible. Sertoli cells nurse the sperm and form the blood-testis barrier that hides them from your immune system; Leydig cells make testosterone when LH tells them to, while FSH drives the Sertoli cells. The process takes about 70 days, ending in spermiogenesis, where a round cell sheds cytoplasm and becomes a head with an enzyme-filled acrosome, a mitochondria-packed midpiece and tail.

Those sperm still cannot swim; motility comes over about two weeks in the epididymis. The seminal vesicles add alkaline, fructose-rich fluid, the prostate adds slightly acidic fluid with enzymes that thin it, and the bulbourethral glands neutralize the urethra — which is why most of semen is gland secretion, and a vasectomy barely changes the volume.

Common misconceptions

  • "Reabsorption and secretion are the same." Reabsorption: filtrate → blood. Secretion: blood → filtrate.
  • "An ovum is what gets ovulated." A secondary oocyte is. It becomes an ovum only if fertilized.
  • "FSH and LH fall in menopause." They rise — the ovaries stop responding, so negative feedback is lost.
