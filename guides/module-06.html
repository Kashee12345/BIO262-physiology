Topic walkthrough — read this first

The action potential, step by step

At rest the inside of a neuron sits at about −70 mV, held there by the sodium–potassium pump. A stimulus nudges it upward. Nothing happens until it reaches threshold at about −55 mV — and then everything happens at once, identically, every time. That is what "all-or-none" means.

At threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels fly open and Na⁺ rushes in, driving the inside to about +30 mV: depolarization. Those channels then inactivate and potassium channels open, so K⁺ moves out and the inside falls negative again: repolarization. Potassium is slow to close, so it overshoots to about −90 mV: hyperpolarization. The pump then restores the resting state.

Sodium in, potassium out. If you remember nothing else from this module, remember that.

Protecting and supplying the brain

Three membranes cover the brain — dura (tough, outer), arachnoid (web-like, middle), and pia (delicate, hugging the surface). Cerebrospinal fluid circulates in the subarachnoid space, cushioning the brain and making it effectively buoyant so it does not crush under its own weight.

Gray matter is cell bodies and does the processing. White matter is myelinated axons and does the transmitting — it looks white because myelin is fatty.

Who does what in the brain

Frontal lobe: decisions, planning, personality, voluntary movement. Parietal: touch, pressure, pain, temperature. Temporal: hearing, language comprehension, memory. Occipital: vision. Cerebellum: coordination and balance — damage causes clumsiness, not paralysis. Brainstem: breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, which is why brainstem injury is so often fatal. Thalamus: the relay station nearly all sensory input passes through. Hypothalamus: hunger, thirst, temperature, and the bridge to the endocrine system.

Two settings for the body

The sympathetic division is fight-or-flight: heart rate up, blood pressure up, airways open, pupils dilate, digestion suspended. The parasympathetic division is rest-and-digest and does the reverse.

Neither is good or bad. They are two settings, and most organs receive both. Losing parasympathetic tone does not make you calm — it leaves you tachycardic.

Smell and taste

Both need the chemical to dissolve first. In olfaction, dissolved odorants bind receptors on the olfactory epithelium, bipolar neurons fire, and the signal reaches the olfactory bulb and then the cortex — bypassing the thalamus entirely, the only sense that does. That direct line to the limbic system is why a smell can drop you into a memory.

Taste has five types: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Spicy is not among them — capsaicin triggers pain and heat receptors, not taste buds.

How a neuron decides to fire

Before any action potential exists, the neuron is adding up. Neurotransmitters open channels that produce graded potentials — small local voltage changes that fade with distance. An EPSP nudges the membrane toward threshold; an IPSP pushes it away. They combine by temporal summation when one synapse fires rapidly in succession, and by spatial summation when several synapses fire at once.

The running total arrives at the axon hillock, the trigger zone, where voltage-gated sodium channels sit densest. Reach threshold — about minus 55 millivolts — and an action potential fires at full size. Fall short and nothing happens at all. That is the all-or-none rule, which is why a stronger stimulus never makes a bigger impulse; it makes more per second, and frequency is how intensity is encoded.

Refractory periods, and why signals go one way

Nothing physically stops an impulse spreading backwards along the axon except time. During the absolute refractory period the sodium channels behind the impulse are inactivated rather than simply closed, and no stimulus of any strength will reopen them until the membrane repolarizes. The membrane just travelled through is therefore deaf, so the signal can only move forward into resting membrane.

The relative refractory period follows, while the membrane is still repolarizing and often hyperpolarized past resting because potassium is still leaving. Some sodium channels have reset, so firing is possible but needs a stronger stimulus than usual. Keep the directions straight: sodium in depolarizes, potassium out repolarizes. The absolute period also sets a ceiling on how fast a neuron can fire, which is why frequency coding cannot rise without limit.

Myelin, and what demyelination costs

Two things set conduction speed. The first is axon diameter — a wider axon offers less resistance to current, the way a wide pipe moves more water. The second, and far more powerful, is myelin: fatty insulation laid down by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system, which stops charge leaking out through the membrane.

Voltage-gated sodium channels cluster in the bare gaps between wraps, the nodes of Ranvier, so the impulse is regenerated only at the nodes and effectively jumps between them. That is saltatory conduction. Strip that myelin, as multiple sclerosis does in the central nervous system, and current bleeds away between nodes, so conduction slows or fails outright — which is why the symptoms are weakness, numbness and visual loss.

Gray matter, white matter, and why the pattern flips

Gray matter is cell bodies, dendrites and synapses, so it is where processing happens. White matter is myelinated axons, white because of the myelin, so it is where information travels. In the cerebrum and cerebellum the gray sits outside as cortex with white beneath, but in the spinal cord it inverts — gray in a central butterfly, white tracts wrapped around it.

White matter runs three ways: association fibers within one hemisphere, commissural fibers crossing between the hemispheres, mostly through the corpus callosum, and projection fibers running between cortex and cord. Deep gray includes the basal nuclei and the thalamus, which relays every sensory pathway to the cortex except olfaction. Smell reaches cortex directly, which is why an odor can summon a memory before you can name it.

The autonomic system at the receptor level

Both divisions use a two-neuron chain, and in both the preganglionic neuron releases acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors in a ganglion — that much is identical. The difference comes afterwards. Parasympathetic fibers are craniosacral, with long preganglionic and short postganglionic neurons, ending in acetylcholine on muscarinic receptors. Sympathetic fibers are thoracolumbar, short then long, ending in norepinephrine on alpha and beta adrenergic receptors, with sweat glands the exception, taking acetylcholine on muscarinic receptors.

The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion whose cells have no axons, so instead of targeting one organ they pour epinephrine into the blood. Neither division is good or bad; both run constantly as autonomic tone, which is why blocking either is a drug strategy — beta blockers for a racing heart, atropine for a dangerously slow one.

Common misconceptions

  • "Depolarization is potassium moving in." Depolarization is sodium in. Repolarization is potassium out.
  • "Sympathetic is bad, parasympathetic is good." Both are essential and balanced.
  • "All senses relay through the thalamus." Olfaction is the exception — it bypasses the thalamus entirely.
