High-yield facts

  • Carbohydrate digestion starts in the mouth; protein in the stomach; fat mainly in the small intestine.
  • Bile emulsifies (mechanical); pancreatic lipase digests (chemical). Bile is not an enzyme.
  • Jejunum = main absorption. Ileum = vitamin B12, bile salts, fat-soluble vitamins.
  • CCK ← fat/protein (contracts gallbladder). Secretin ← acid (releases bicarbonate).

Topic walkthrough — read this first

Two kinds of digestion, all the way down

Mechanical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces without changing it chemically — chewing, churning in the stomach, segmentation in the small intestine. Its purpose is surface area, because enzymes can only work on surfaces. Chemical digestion breaks the actual bonds.

Movement matters too. Peristalsis propels food forward; segmentation mixes it without moving it along. When you meet a GI term, ask which of those two it is.

Where each nutrient gets dismantled

Carbohydrate starts in the mouth with salivary amylase — the only chemical digestion that happens there. Protein starts in the stomach, where HCl denatures it and activates pepsinogen into pepsin. Fat is barely touched until the small intestine.

In the duodenum everything arrives at once: pancreatic amylase for carbohydrate, proteases for protein, pancreatic lipase for fat, and bicarbonate to neutralize the acid so those enzymes can work at all.

Bile is not an enzyme — and this is the exam's favorite trap

Fat does not dissolve in water, so it arrives as large globules that enzymes can barely reach. Bile, made by the liver and stored and concentrated by the gallbladder, breaks those globules into tiny droplets. That is emulsification — a mechanical act. It changes surface area, not chemistry.

Pancreatic lipase then does the actual chemical digestion of those droplets. Bile prepares; lipase digests. Keep the two verbs separate and this section becomes easy.

Absorption has addresses

The duodenum takes iron, calcium and magnesium and does most of the chemical work. The jejunum is the main absorptive site — carbohydrate, protein, fat, and water-soluble vitamins — thanks to folds, villi and microvilli multiplying its surface area. The ileum is the specialist: vitamin B12 (with intrinsic factor from the stomach), bile salts for recycling, and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. The colon reclaims water and electrolytes and secretes no enzymes of its own — its bacteria do any remaining work and make vitamin K.

Notice that intrinsic factor is made in the stomach but used in the ileum. That is why a stomach problem shows up as a blood problem.

How a meal actually moves

You can break a meal down perfectly and still get nowhere if it does not move. Mastication breaks food and mixes it with saliva into a bolus. Deglutition follows in three phases — a voluntary buccal phase, then involuntary pharyngeal and esophageal phases in which the larynx lifts and the epiglottis folds over the airway.

Peristalsis then propels: circular muscle contracts behind the bolus and relaxes ahead of it. In the small intestine segmentation takes over — it does not push forward, it mixes chyme against the absorptive wall. The colon adds slow haustral churning and a few daily mass movements. Sphincters gate every handoff — upper esophageal, lower esophageal, pyloric, ileocaecal and both anal — which is why a weak lower esophageal sphincter lets acid into a tube with no acid defenses.

The four organs that never touch food

Four accessory organs do the chemical heavy lifting without food ever touching them — secretions travel down ducts.

The salivary glands — parotid, submandibular, sublingual — supply water, mucus, lysozyme and amylase to start starch. The liver makes bile continuously, but that is its smallest job: nutrient-rich blood from the gut reaches it through the hepatic portal vein, so the liver stores glycogen, builds albumin and clotting factors, converts ammonia to urea, and clears the drugs you give before they reach the systemic circulation. The gallbladder stores and concentrates bile until CCK squeezes it out. The pancreas is two organs — acinar cells send enzymes and duct cells bicarbonate to the duodenum, islets send insulin and glucagon to blood. Which is why one stone at the shared duct outlet causes jaundice and pancreatitis together.

The three phases, and the four hormones

Secretion is timed, not constant — three phases run the clock.

The cephalic phase starts before food arrives: the sight, smell or thought of food drives vagal output and your stomach begins secreting. The gastric phase begins with stretch and peptides, which release gastrin from G cells and drive acid and motility hard. The intestinal phase mostly switches the stomach off. Acid entering the duodenum releases secretin from S cells, which calls pancreatic bicarbonate to neutralize it. Fat and protein release CCK from I cells, which orders pancreatic enzymes, contracts the gallbladder and slows gastric emptying. GIP from the duodenum starts insulin release early, before absorbed glucose fully reaches the blood. Which is why a fatty meal sits heavy — CCK is deliberately holding it back until the duodenum can keep up.

Why the stomach does not digest itself

Stomach contents sit near pH 2 — acid strong enough to digest the tissue producing it. Four defenses stop that.

Surface cells secrete an alkaline mucus layer that traps bicarbonate against the epithelium, so the cell surface itself stays near neutral. Tight junctions stop acid leaking between cells. The lining is replaced every three to six days. And the dangerous enzymes are secreted inactive: chief cells release pepsinogen, a zymogen that only becomes pepsin once HCl meets it out in the lumen — never inside the cell that made it. The pancreas uses the same trick with trypsinogen. Prostaglandins maintain that mucus and its blood supply, which is why the NSAIDs you hand out, by blocking prostaglandins, cause ulcers, and why H. pylori, which burrows beneath the mucus, does too.

What the colon is actually for

By the time chyme reaches the colon the digesting is finished — the colon secretes mucus and no enzymes at all.

Its work is reclamation and storage. About a liter and a half of liquid chyme arrives each day, and the colon reclaims nearly all of that water along with sodium and chloride, leaving formed stool. Its microbiota ferment the fiber your own enzymes cannot touch, producing short-chain fatty acids, gas, and usable vitamin K and several B vitamins. Haustral churning holds contents against the wall, and two or three mass movements a day drive stool into the rectum, where stretch triggers the defecation reflex — internal sphincter involuntary, external sphincter yours. Which is why diarrhea dehydrates so quickly: transit outruns reabsorption, and potassium leaves with the water.

Common misconceptions

  • "Bile is an enzyme." Bile emulsifies — a mechanical action. Pancreatic lipase does the chemical digestion.
  • "Most absorption happens in the stomach." Very little. The jejunum is the main absorptive site.
  • "The colon secretes digestive enzymes." None. Its bacteria do any remaining breakdown.
