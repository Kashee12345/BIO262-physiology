Module 1 — Physiology, homeostasis and chemistry

Fifteen questions. Everything here builds on one idea: the body holds itself steady, and chemistry is how it does it.

1Define physiology. Give an example of cell, organ, and systemic physiology. What is pathophysiology?

Physiology is the study of how the body works. Anatomy is the parts list; physiology is the operating manual.

  • Cell physiology — how a neuron holds a charge at rest and then fires.
  • Organ physiology — how the heart fills and then ejects blood in one beat.
  • Systemic physiology — how the cardiovascular system as a whole keeps blood pressure steady.

Pathophysiology is the study of how those functions change when there is disease. It is physiology gone wrong.

2What is homeostasis? How do the nervous system and endocrine system regulate it?

Homeostasis is keeping the inside of the body steady while the outside changes. Temperature near 37°C, blood pH 7.35 to 7.45, and blood sugar, calcium and oxygen all stay in narrow ranges.

  • Nervous system — fast and electrical. Signals travel in milliseconds and stop just as fast.
  • Endocrine system — slower and chemical. Hormones travel in blood, take seconds to hours, and keep working.

They work together. Nerves start a response, hormones keep it going.

3Write an example of negative feedback – include stimulus, receptor, control center, effector, result. What does the outcome do to the receptor?

Example: you get too warm.

  • Stimulus — body temperature rises above the set point.
  • Receptor — temperature receptors in the skin and hypothalamus notice.
  • Control center — the hypothalamus compares it to the set point and decides.
  • Effector — sweat glands make sweat, and skin blood vessels widen.
  • Result — heat is lost and temperature falls back to normal.

What it does to the receptor: it shuts the receptor's signal off. The response removes the thing that started it. That is why it is called negative — the response opposes the change. Negative means opposing, not bad.

4Write an example of positive feedback – how is this outcome different than negative feedback? What does it do to the receptor?

Example: blood clotting. Platelets stick to a damaged vessel and release chemicals that call in more platelets, which release more chemicals.

Other examples: labor contractions, and the LH surge that triggers ovulation.

How it differs: the response builds on the change instead of opposing it. The value moves further away from where it started, not back toward it.

What it does to the receptor: it increases the receptor's signal, so the loop feeds itself. Something outside the loop has to stop it — the sealed vessel, or the birth of the baby.

5What does pH measure? What does a pH of 2 mean? What about 9? Describe the relationship between pH and hydrogen ion concentration.

pH measures how many free hydrogen ions (H⁺) are in a solution — how acidic or basic it is.

  • pH 2 is strongly acidic. Lots of H⁺. Stomach acid sits down here.
  • pH 9 is basic, or alkaline. Very little H⁺.
  • pH 7 is neutral.

The relationship is inverse: more H⁺ means a lower pH, less H⁺ means a higher pH.

The scale is logarithmic, so each whole number is a tenfold change. pH 2 has 10 times more H⁺ than pH 3, and 100 times more than pH 4.
6What is the pH of blood? What is it called when pH of the blood is above the set point? Below the set point?

Blood pH is 7.35 to 7.45, so slightly basic. Call it 7.4.

  • Above 7.45 — alkalosis.
  • Below 7.35 — acidosis.

That window is tight for a reason. Outside it, proteins change shape and stop working, and enzymes, receptors and channels are all proteins.

7How does a buffer keep pH from swinging when acid is added? Which buffer pair does most of that work in blood?

A buffer is a chemical pair that resists a change in pH. It soaks up extra hydrogen ions when there are too many and releases them when there are too few.

The main example is the bicarbonate buffer system: CO₂ + H₂O ↔ H₂CO₃ ↔ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻.

Others are the phosphate buffer inside cells, and proteins such as hemoglobin and albumin.

The lungs back it up by changing how much CO₂ you blow off, and the kidneys by handling bicarbonate and hydrogen ions.

8Where are electrons found in an atom?

In the space around the nucleus, arranged in energy levels called shells.

  • Nucleus — protons (positive) and neutrons (no charge).
  • Around it — electrons (negative), moving in shells.
9What is a valence shell? What are valence electrons?

The valence shell is the outermost energy level of an atom. The valence electrons are the electrons sitting in it.

They matter because they decide how, and whether, an atom bonds. An atom is stable when its valence shell is full — eight electrons for most atoms in the body, two for hydrogen.

10What is an ionic bond? What's happening to electrons? Draw an ionic bond.

An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another atom.

The atom that loses electrons becomes positive. The atom that gains them becomes negative. Opposite charges attract, and that attraction is the bond.

Classic example: sodium gives its one valence electron to chlorine, making Na⁺ and Cl⁻, which stick together as table salt.

Drawing it: show sodium with 1 electron in its outer shell and chlorine with 7. Draw an arrow moving that electron from sodium to chlorine. Then label the result Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and show them attracted to each other.
11What is an ion? What is the difference between a cation and an anion?

An ion is an atom that carries an electrical charge because it has lost or gained electrons.

  • Cation — positive, because it lost electrons. Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺.
  • Anion — negative, because it gained electrons. Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻.
A way to keep them straight: an anion is a negative ion.
12What is happening with electrons in a covalent bond? How do polar and nonpolar bonds differ? Draw a polar covalent bond and a nonpolar covalent bond.

In a covalent bond the atoms share electrons instead of transferring them.

  • Nonpolar covalent — shared equally, because both atoms pull about the same. Example: O₂, or the carbon-hydrogen bonds in fats.
  • Polar covalent — shared unequally, because one atom pulls harder. That atom becomes slightly negative and the other slightly positive. Example: the oxygen-hydrogen bonds in water.
Drawing them: for nonpolar, draw the shared pair of electrons sitting midway between the two atoms. For polar, draw the shared pair closer to oxygen, then mark oxygen δ⁻ and hydrogen δ⁺.
13What is a hydrogen bond? What relevance does this have in the human body?

A hydrogen bond is a weak attraction between two polar molecules. The slightly positive hydrogen on one is pulled toward a slightly negative atom on the other. That atom is usually oxygen or nitrogen.

One is weak. Many together are strong.

Why it matters in the body:

  • Holds water molecules to each other. That is what gives water its surface tension, and why it resists temperature swings.
  • Holds the two strands of DNA together, weakly enough that they can be pulled apart to be read.
  • Holds proteins in their folded shape. Break those bonds with heat or a pH swing and the protein denatures, so it stops working.
14What bond makes a single water molecule?

Polar covalent bonds. Oxygen shares electrons with two hydrogens, but pulls them closer to itself, so oxygen ends up slightly negative and the hydrogens slightly positive.

15What bond is formed between multiple water molecules?

Hydrogen bonds. The slightly positive hydrogen of one water molecule is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen of another.

Worth keeping straight: inside one water molecule the bonds are polar covalent. Between water molecules they are hydrogen bonds.

Module 2 — The cell, transport and protein synthesis

Twenty questions. The membrane decides what gets in, and the organelles decide what happens next.

1What is the role of the cell membrane? What does it mean when we say the cell membrane is selectively permeable?

The cell membrane separates the inside of the cell from the outside and controls what crosses.

Selectively permeable means it lets some things through and blocks others.

  • Cross easily — small, uncharged, fat-soluble things: oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones.
  • Cannot cross alone — charged or large things: sodium, potassium, calcium, glucose, amino acids. These need a protein to let them through.
2Describe the following membrane proteins: anchoring, cell adhesion, enzyme, transport, antigen.
  • Anchoring — attaches the membrane to the cell's internal skeleton or to structures outside, giving the cell shape and stability.
  • Cell adhesion — holds cells to each other or to surrounding material, forming junctions.
  • Enzyme — speeds up a chemical reaction right at the membrane.
  • Transport — forms the channels and carriers that move substances across.
  • Antigen — an identity marker on the surface that lets the immune system tell your cells from foreign ones.
3What type of transport does NOT require energy (ATP)? How does this relate to the concentration gradient?

Passive transport needs no ATP.

It moves substances down the concentration gradient — from where there is more to where there is less. The gradient itself supplies the energy, so the cell pays nothing.

Passive types: simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis and filtration.

4What is diffusion? What is moving? How is it moving in relation to the concentration gradient?

Diffusion is movement of a dissolved substance from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated.

  • What moves: the solute, meaning the dissolved substance.
  • Direction: down the concentration gradient, until it is evenly spread out.

This is the whole basis of gas exchange — oxygen and carbon dioxide simply diffuse down their gradients.

5What is the difference between simple diffusion and facilitated diffusion? Does either type of diffusion use energy (ATP)?
  • Simple diffusion — the substance passes straight through the membrane on its own. Works for small, uncharged, fat-soluble things like oxygen and carbon dioxide.
  • Facilitated diffusion — the substance goes through a channel or carrier protein, because it cannot cross the membrane by itself. Glucose and ions travel this way.

Neither uses ATP. Both are passive, and both run down the concentration gradient. The only difference is whether a protein is needed to get through.

6What is osmosis? What is moving? How is it moving in relation to the concentration gradient?

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane.

  • What moves: water, not the solute.
  • Direction: water moves toward the side with more solute, which is the side with less water. So water is still moving down its own gradient.
The sentence that answers most tonicity questions: water follows solute.
7Does osmosis require ATP?

No. Osmosis is passive. Water moves down its own gradient and the cell spends nothing.

8What type of transport uses ATP? List 2 examples.

Active transport uses ATP, because it moves a substance against its gradient — from low concentration to high.

  • Sodium-potassium pump — pushes 3 sodium out and pulls 2 potassium in for each ATP. This is what keeps sodium low and potassium high inside the cell.
  • Calcium pump — moves calcium out of the cytoplasm, back into storage or out of the cell.

Bulk transport also costs ATP: endocytosis brings large material in, exocytosis pushes it out.

9What job does the nucleus do, and what is the nucleolus inside it for?

Holds the DNA and directs the cell. This is where DNA is copied into mRNA during transcription. It also contains the nucleolus, where ribosomes are put together.

10What does a ribosome build, and where in the cell are ribosomes found?

Builds proteins. It reads mRNA and links amino acids together in the order the message specifies. Ribosomes float free in the cytoplasm or sit attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum.

11A protein arrives at the Golgi apparatus. What happens to it there?

Receives proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum. It then modifies, sorts and packages them into vesicles, and ships them where they need to go — inside the cell or out of it.

Think of it as the shipping department: it does not make the product, it finishes and addresses it.
12What does a lysosome contain, and what does it use that for?

Holds digestive enzymes. It breaks down worn-out organelles, and material the cell has taken in from outside. It is the cell's recycling and disposal unit.

13How do rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum differ, both in structure and in what they make?
  • Rough ER — covered in ribosomes. Makes and folds proteins that will be exported or built into the membrane.
  • Smooth ER — no ribosomes. Makes lipids and steroids, breaks down drugs and toxins, and stores calcium. In muscle it is the calcium store that triggers contraction.
Rough has ribosomes, so rough handles proteins. Smooth has none, so smooth handles fats.
14What does the mitochondrion make, and which cells carry the most of them?

Makes most of the cell's ATP, using oxygen. It is where the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain happen.

Cells with high energy demand are packed with mitochondria. Heart muscle, kidney tubules and neurons are the examples to know. They are also the first to be injured when blood flow fails.

15Draw out the pathway of ATP production and location in the cell starting with glycolysis. Indicate whether the step is aerobic or anaerobic.
  • Glycolysis — in the cytoplasm — anaerobic, no oxygen needed. One glucose becomes 2 pyruvate, with a small net gain of 2 ATP.
  • If there is no oxygen: pyruvate becomes lactic acid, still in the cytoplasm, still anaerobic.
  • If there is oxygen: pyruvate enters the mitochondrion.
  • Citric acid cycle (Krebs) — in the mitochondrial matrix — aerobic. Produces a small amount of ATP and loads up electron carriers.
  • Electron transport chain — on the inner mitochondrial membrane — aerobic. Makes the large majority of the ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, and water is the product.
Drawing it: one arrow chain — glucose → glycolysis (cytoplasm, anaerobic) → pyruvate → then branch. One branch to lactic acid (no oxygen). The other into the mitochondrion → citric acid cycle (matrix, aerobic) → electron transport chain (inner membrane, aerobic) → most of the ATP.
16What is transcription? Where does it happen in the cell?

Transcription is copying a gene's DNA sequence into a strand of messenger RNA.

Where: in the nucleus, because that is where the DNA is.

Transcription is copying the same language, DNA to RNA. Translation changes language, RNA to protein.
17What is translation? Where does it happen in the cell?

Translation is reading the mRNA and building the protein it specifies.

Where: at the ribosome — either floating in the cytoplasm or attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum.

18What are the 3 types of RNA and where are they used in transcription/translation?
  • mRNA (messenger) — made during transcription in the nucleus, then carries the message out to the ribosome to be read during translation.
  • tRNA (transfer) — used in translation. Brings the matching amino acid to the ribosome.
  • rRNA (ribosomal) — makes up the ribosome itself, so it works during translation.
19What does a ribosome do for protein synthesis?

It holds the mRNA and the incoming tRNAs together, so the correct amino acid is added next. It then links those amino acids with peptide bonds to build the growing chain.

In short: the ribosome is the workbench where the message becomes a protein.

20What is mitosis? What is the result of mitosis? Why is this important for life?

Mitosis is division of the nucleus, producing two genetically identical daughter nuclei. Cytokinesis then splits the cytoplasm into two cells.

The result: two cells with the same number and the same kind of chromosomes as the original cell.

Why it matters:

  • Growth — you got from one cell to trillions this way.
  • Repair — damaged tissue is replaced.
  • Replacement — skin, gut lining and blood cells wear out constantly and must be remade.
