Everything explained, module by module

How the eye focuses

The cornea does most of the bending of light; the lens does the fine adjustment. For a near object the ciliary muscle contracts and the lens rounds up, bending light more. For a distant object it flattens. This is accommodation, and it is the reverse of what most students first guess.

As the lens stiffens with age it can no longer round up, which is presbyopia — the reason reading glasses arrive in middle age.

Turning light into a signal

The retina has two photoreceptors with opposite specialities. Rods are extremely light-sensitive but give no color and poor detail, and sit in the periphery — they are your night vision. Cones need bright light but give color and sharp detail, and cluster at the fovea, which is why you turn your eyes toward whatever you want to see clearly.

Light strikes opsin, which changes shape and starts a cascade. The signal passes photoreceptor → bipolar cell → ganglion cell, and ganglion cell axons leave the eye as the optic nerve. Where they exit there are no photoreceptors at all — your blind spot.

Hearing is a chain of energy conversions

Sound starts as pressure waves in air. The pinna collects them. The tympanic membrane converts them into mechanical vibration. The three ossicles amplify that vibration and drive the oval window. That moves fluid inside the cochlea, which bends hair cells, and there the mechanical becomes electrical. Cranial nerve VIII carries it to the temporal lobe.

Break the chain early — wax, a perforated drum, stiff ossicles — and you get conductive loss. Break it at the hair cells or nerve and you get sensorineural loss.

Balance: two different questions

The inner ear answers two separate questions with two separate structures. Am I tilted, or accelerating in a straight line? is answered by the utricle and saccule, where calcium crystals called otoliths sit on a gel and drag on hair cells when gravity or acceleration pulls them.

Am I rotating? is answered by the three semicircular canals, set at right angles to each other. When your head turns, the fluid inside lags behind and bends hair cells in the ampulla. That lag is also why you still feel like you are spinning for a few seconds after you stop.

Where the image goes after the retina

Light becomes a signal in the retina, but you do not see anything until the occipital lobe assembles it. Axons of the ganglion cells leave the eye as the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm only the fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross, while the temporal fibers stay on their own side, so each optic tract carries the opposite half of the visual world rather than the opposite eye.

The signal then relays in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and travels through the optic radiations to the primary visual cortex. Damage before the chiasm blinds one eye; damage at the chiasm — classically a pituitary tumor — removes both outer fields, which is why such a patient clips door frames on both sides.

Color, detail, and seeing in the dark

Where photoreceptors sit matters as much as how they work. Cones come in three kinds, tuned to short, medium and long wavelengths, and your brain reads color from the ratio of their firing, which is why losing one type distorts color rather than removing it. Cones crowd the fovea centralis, each wired almost one to one, giving fine detail but only in good light.

Rods pack the periphery and funnel many receptors onto one pathway, summing faint light into a usable signal at the cost of detail. Their rhodopsin bleaches the moment light hits it and takes twenty to thirty minutes to rebuild — which is why a dark room looks black when you first walk in, and why a faint star is easier to see just off to one side.

When the image lands in the wrong place

A normal eye focuses light on the retina itself. In myopia the eyeball is too long, so the image forms in front of the retina and distance blurs — a diverging concave lens pushes it back. In hyperopia the eyeball is too short, so the image forms behind the retina and near work blurs — a converging convex lens pulls it forward. Astigmatism is uneven corneal curvature, so nothing focuses sharply at any distance.

Presbyopia is different: the ageing lens stiffens and cannot round up for near vision, which is why reading glasses arrive in your forties. Acuity compares you with a normal eye: 20/200 means you read at twenty feet what a normal eye reads at two hundred, so a bigger bottom number is worse vision, not better.

How the cochlea codes pitch and loudness

Pitch and volume are carried by two different features of the same wave. The basilar membrane is narrow and stiff at the base and wide and floppy at the apex, so high frequencies peak near the base and low frequencies near the apex. Whichever region vibrates most bends the stereocilia of its hair cells against the tectorial membrane, and the temporal lobe reads pitch from which fibers of cranial nerve VIII fired. Loudness is those cells bending further and firing faster.

That anatomy also sorts hearing loss. Conductive loss blocks the chain before the receptors — wax, middle ear fluid, a fixed ossicle — so clearing it restores hearing. Sensorineural loss is dead hair cells or nerve, from noise, ageing or ototoxic drugs, and because hair cells never regrow it is permanent.

What the vestibular receptors can and cannot feel

Every vestibular receptor is a hair cell; what sits on top decides what it detects. In the three semicircular canals — one per plane of space — hair cells of the crista ampullaris project into a gel cupula, and only moving fluid bends it. When your head starts or stops rotating the endolymph lags behind and deflects the cupula, which makes these canals detectors of angular acceleration and nothing else.

Gravity and linear acceleration are the job of the utricle and saccule, whose maculae are capped by otoliths — crystals heavy enough to sag when you tilt or pull away in a car. Stop spinning and the endolymph keeps moving, so your canals still report turning while your eyes report stillness — which is why the room seems to spin.

Receptors decide the speed of everything

A hormone can only do what its receptor allows, and receptor type sets the timescale.

Ion channel-linked receptors are channels — binding opens them and ions flow immediately. This is the fastest option and it is what the neuromuscular junction uses. G-protein coupled receptors sit in the membrane and trigger second messengers inside, which amplifies enormously: one hormone molecule can produce thousands of downstream events. Enzyme-linked receptors activate intracellular enzymes.

Intracellular receptors are the outlier. Lipid-soluble hormones — steroids and thyroid hormone — cross the membrane and bind receptors inside, and the complex alters gene transcription. That takes hours, but the effect lasts. Water-soluble hormones cannot cross, so they must work from the surface. That single distinction predicts almost everything about how a hormone behaves.

Feedback is the whole subject

Nearly every endocrine axis is a negative feedback loop with three levels: hypothalamus → pituitary → target gland. The hypothalamus releases a releasing hormone, the anterior pituitary releases a tropic hormone, the target gland releases the final hormone — and that final hormone shuts off the two above it.

This is why lab interpretation works. High T4 with low TSH means the thyroid is overproducing and correctly suppressing the pituitary — primary hyperthyroidism. Students who expect both to move together get these questions wrong every time.

The hormones worth knowing cold

Group them by what they defend. Glucose: insulin lowers it (beta cells), glucagon raises it (alpha cells). Calcium: PTH raises, calcitonin lowers. Water and salt: ADH keeps water, aldosterone keeps sodium. Metabolism: T3 and T4. Stress: cortisol and epinephrine. Reproduction: FSH grows the follicle, LH triggers ovulation — the pair most often confused.

For each, learn the trigger and the effect as a sentence, not as two separate facts.

When the axis breaks

Endocrine disease is nearly always too much or too little of one hormone, and the clinical picture is just that hormone's normal job turned up or down.

Too much cortisol gives Cushing: high glucose, central weight gain, thin skin, weak bone — cortisol's ordinary effects, exaggerated. Too little gives Addison: fatigue, low blood pressure, low sodium, high potassium. Too much thyroid hormone speeds everything — weight loss, heat intolerance, fast heart. Too little slows everything — weight gain, cold intolerance, slow heart.

This is why you rarely need to memorize symptom lists. Work out what the hormone does, then push it in either direction.

Why steroids must be tapered

Give someone cortisol from outside for long enough and their own axis notices. Negative feedback shuts down CRH and ACTH, and the adrenal cortex, unused, shrinks.

Stop the drug suddenly and there is now no cortisol from either source — the tablets have gone and the gland cannot restart quickly. That is an adrenal crisis: hypotension, shock, potentially death. Tapering gives the axis weeks to wake back up. It is negative feedback with real consequences.

The three signals that make a gland fire

Glands do not secrete at random — something has to tell them to. There are only three kinds of trigger, and knowing which one a hormone uses tells you how fast it responds.

Humoral stimuli are changes in a blood value the gland itself measures. Parathyroid cells read falling calcium and release PTH; beta cells read rising glucose and release insulin. Neural stimuli are direct nerve input — sympathetic fibers firing onto the adrenal medulla dump epinephrine into the blood within seconds. Hormonal stimuli are one hormone triggering another, which is the whole hypothalamic-pituitary pattern: a releasing hormone tells the pituitary, the pituitary tells the target gland.

That layering is why your heart races almost instantly in a fright but cortisol, which travels the long hormonal route, takes many minutes to rise.

Sensory receptors, sorted by what they detect

The same word, receptor, covers two different things, and the exam expects both. Hormone receptors are proteins that bind a chemical messenger. Sensory receptors are structures that convert a stimulus into an electrical signal your nervous system can read, and they are classified by what they detect.

Mechanoreceptors respond to physical deformation — touch, stretch, the baroreceptors reading blood pressure, proprioceptors reporting joint position, and the hair cells of hearing and balance. Thermoreceptors detect temperature, nociceptors detect tissue damage as pain, chemoreceptors detect chemicals including taste, smell, blood gases and the osmoreceptors watching blood concentration, and photoreceptors detect light.

Most adapt — you stop noticing your watch within minutes. Nociceptors barely adapt at all, which is why pain keeps shouting until you deal with the cause.

How a hormone travels sets how long it lasts

A hormone reaches every cell, but how it travels depends on whether it dissolves in water or in fat.

Water-soluble hormones — the peptides and the catecholamines — dissolve straight into plasma, cannot cross the fatty cell membrane, and must bind surface receptors. Enzymes, the liver and the kidneys clear them within minutes. Lipid-soluble hormones — the steroids from the adrenal cortex and gonads, plus thyroid hormone — barely dissolve in plasma at all, so they travel bound to carrier proteins with only the small free fraction active. Being fat-soluble, they enter the cell and bind intracellular receptors, changing gene transcription directly.

Bound hormone is a reservoir, so steroid and thyroid half-lives run hours to days — which is why levothyroxine is a single daily tablet while insulin must be redosed or infused.

Why the same hormone level stops working

A hormone circulates everywhere, but only cells with the matching receptor respond — the receptor, not the hormone, defines the target tissue.

Cells adjust that sensitivity. Persistently high hormone levels cause down-regulation: the cell withdraws receptors, so the same level produces less effect. Scarce hormone causes up-regulation, making the cell more sensitive. Type 2 diabetes is that pattern — insulin is present, but the tissues answer weakly, through fewer receptors and faulty internal signaling.

Hormones interact three ways. Permissive means one hormone must be present for another to work fully — epinephrine needs thyroid hormone. Synergistic means two push the same way and amplify each other beyond a simple sum — glucagon, cortisol and epinephrine on blood glucose. Antagonistic means opposition — insulin against glucagon. That is why an untreated hypothyroid patient handles stress badly.

One pituitary, two completely different glands

The pituitary looks like one gland and behaves like two.

The anterior pituitary is true glandular tissue. It manufactures six hormones — GH, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH and prolactin — controlled by releasing and inhibiting hormones arriving from the hypothalamus down a short private set of portal veins. FSH and LH are not interchangeable: FSH grows ovarian follicles and supports sperm production, while LH triggers ovulation and stimulates testosterone. TSH is pituitary output, not thyroid output — so a low TSH usually means the pituitary stopped shouting because thyroid hormone is already high.

The posterior pituitary manufactures nothing. ADH and oxytocin are made by hypothalamic neurons and stored in their nerve endings — which is why stalk damage can cut ADH off from the bloodstream and cause diabetes insipidus even with the lobe intact.

What blood is for

Blood transports, regulates and protects. It carries oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste; it regulates temperature by shifting where blood flows and helps hold pH steady; and it protects through immunity and clotting.

It is about 55% plasma and 45% cells. The main plasma protein is albumin, and its job is osmotic: it holds fluid inside the vessel. Lose albumin and fluid leaks into tissue — edema.

The cells, and what each one is for

Erythrocytes carry oxygen on hemoglobin. They lose their nucleus to make room, which maximizes capacity but caps their lifespan at about 120 days.

Leukocytes defend, and each has a speciality. Neutrophils are the most numerous and arrive first at bacterial infection. Eosinophils handle parasites and allergy. Basophils release histamine and drive inflammation. Lymphocytes run adaptive immunity. Monocytes leave the blood and become tissue macrophages.

Platelets form the initial plug and provide the surface on which clotting happens.

Vessels and pressure

Every vessel has three layers, but the middle one does the work: the tunica media is smooth muscle, and its contraction or relaxation sets vessel diameter. Narrow the vessel and resistance rises, so pressure rises. Widen it and pressure falls.

Arterioles matter most here — they have the most muscle relative to their size, so they are the main resistance vessels and the main determinant of blood pressure. Capillaries are one cell thick for exchange. Veins are low-pressure and need valves plus skeletal muscle compression to push blood back uphill.

Stopping bleeding, in three steps

One: vascular spasm. The vessel constricts immediately and cuts flow.
Two: platelet plug. Von Willebrand factor bridges platelets onto exposed collagen and they aggregate into a temporary seal.
Three: coagulation. A cascade converts fibrinogen into fibrin, weaving a mesh that traps cells and turns the soft plug into a real clot.

The cascade has two entrances — the intrinsic pathway from damage inside the vessel and the extrinsic pathway from tissue injury outside — and they converge at Factor X. From there it is one road: Factor X → prothrombin becomes thrombin → thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin. Later, plasmin dissolves the clot once the vessel has healed.

What plasma proteins actually do

Blood is roughly 55 percent plasma, and plasma is about 92 percent water — but the small protein fraction, about 7 percent, does most of the specialized work. Albumin is the most abundant, and it generates the colloid osmotic pressure that pulls fluid back into the capillary at its venous end; it also ferries fatty acids, calcium and many drugs.

Globulins come in three families: alpha and beta transport iron, lipids and hormones, while gamma globulins are your antibodies. Fibrinogen is the soluble precursor that thrombin converts into fibrin. All of these except the gamma globulins, which come from plasma cells, are made in the liver — which is why liver failure or severe malnutrition produces edema and easy bleeding at the same time.

The life and death of a red cell

A red cell has no nucleus and no mitochondria — it gave both up for hemoglobin, and that trade leaves it unable to repair itself. So it survives roughly 120 days, then macrophages in the spleen and liver take it apart.

Nothing is wasted. Globin returns to the amino acid pool, iron is carried back on transferrin or stored as ferritin, and the leftover heme ring becomes biliverdin and then bilirubin, which the liver conjugates and excretes in bile. Unconjugated bilirubin is not water soluble and cannot leave in urine, so a failing liver or blocked duct backs it up and yellows skin and sclerae. Replacement is driven by erythropoietin from the kidney whenever oxygen falls — which is why chronic kidney disease causes an anemia that iron alone cannot fix.

Two pathways into one cascade

Vascular spasm comes first and the platelet plug second — coagulation is the third step, and it is what turns a fragile plug into a clot that holds. It has two entry points. The extrinsic pathway starts outside the vessel, when damaged tissue exposes tissue factor, and it works in seconds. The intrinsic pathway starts inside the vessel with contact activation and takes minutes.

Both converge on factor X, the head of the common pathway: activated X builds prothrombinase, which converts prothrombin into thrombin, and thrombin turns soluble fibrinogen into fibrin threads that factor XIII cross-links into a mesh. Factors II, VII, IX and X depend on vitamin K — which is exactly the step warfarin blocks, and why those patients have an INR checked rather than a bleeding time.

Why blood does not clot everywhere

Blood sits against the vessel wall constantly and does not clot, and that takes active work. Intact endothelium is smooth and releases nitric oxide and prostacyclin, which keep platelets from sticking. Circulating antithrombin III inactivates thrombin, heparin from basophils and mast cells accelerates that reaction, and protein C with protein S switches off the amplifying factors.

Once repair is underway, plasminogen trapped inside the clot is converted to plasmin by tissue plasminogen activator, and plasmin digests the fibrin mesh away — fibrinolysis. Clotting is therefore a balance, not a switch, which is why stasis, vessel injury and hypercoagulability — Virchow's triad — can tip a healthy person into a deep vein thrombosis after nothing more dramatic than a long flight.

How blood pressure is regulated

Mean arterial pressure is cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance. Both can change within seconds, but resistance gives the finest control, and it lives in the arterioles, where slight narrowing raises pressure sharply. That word systemic matters: an artery is named for its direction of flow, not its oxygen, and the pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood through a much lower-pressure circuit.

Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch report stretch to the medulla; when pressure falls they fire less, sympathetic outflow rises, vessels constrict and heart rate climbs. Tissues overrule that order locally — carbon dioxide, potassium and low oxygen dilate their own arterioles. Hormones work over hours instead: RAAS, ADH and ANP adjust volume, which is why an older patient whose baroreflex is blunted by antihypertensives faints on standing.

Every misconception on this exam

  • "The lens flattens for near vision." Opposite. It rounds up for near, flattens for far.
  • "20/200 is better than 20/20." Far worse — the larger the second number, the poorer the acuity.
  • "The semicircular canals detect gravity." They detect rotation. The utricle and saccule detect gravity and linear acceleration.
  • "Low TSH means hypothyroid." In primary hyperthyroidism TSH is low because T4 is high — negative feedback.
  • "Steroid hormones bind surface receptors." Lipid-soluble hormones cross the membrane and bind intracellular receptors.
  • "FSH and LH do the same thing." FSH grows the follicle; LH triggers ovulation.
  • "Vascular spasm is the second step of hemostasis." It is first. Platelet plug is second, coagulation third.
  • "Arteries always carry oxygenated blood." The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
  • "Plasma proteins don't matter." Albumin holds fluid in the vessel. Lose it and you get edema.
