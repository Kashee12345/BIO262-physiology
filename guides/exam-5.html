Everything explained, module by module

The heart as a pump

Four chambers, two circuits. The right side receives deoxygenated blood from the body and sends it to the lungs — the pulmonary circuit. The left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pushes it to the body — the systemic circuit. The left ventricle has by far the thickest wall because it has by far the longest push.

Valves make flow one-way. The AV valves (tricuspid right, mitral left) open during diastole to let the ventricles fill and slam shut when the ventricles contract — that closure is S1, the "lub". The semilunar valves (pulmonary and aortic) open during ejection and snap shut as the ventricles relax — that is S2, the "dub". The two heart sounds are simply two sets of valves closing.

Cardiac output, and the two ways to change it

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, around 5 liters a minute at rest. Anything that changes output must change one of those two numbers.

Heart rate is set by autonomic tone — sympathetic speeds it, vagal parasympathetic slows it. Stroke volume depends on preload (how full the ventricle got, which depends on venous return), contractility (how hard it squeezes), and afterload (the pressure it must push against). Stretch a ventricle more and it contracts harder — the Frank–Starling relationship — which is how the heart automatically matches output to return.

The heart as an electrical system

The SA node depolarizes spontaneously and fastest, so it sets the rhythm. The impulse spreads across the atria, then reaches the AV node, which deliberately delays it — that pause is what lets the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract. From there it runs down the Bundle of His, splits into bundle branches, and spreads through the Purkinje fibers so the ventricles contract as one coordinated unit from the apex upward.

On an ECG: P is atrial depolarization, QRS is ventricular depolarization, and T is ventricular repolarization. Atrial repolarization does happen — it is simply buried inside the much larger QRS.

Preload, afterload, contractility

Three words govern stroke volume, and they are easy to keep straight if you picture the ventricle.

Preload is how full it got before it squeezed — it depends on venous return. More filling stretches the fibers, and a stretched ventricle contracts harder. That is the Frank–Starling relationship, and it is how the heart matches output to whatever comes back without needing instructions.

Contractility is how hard it squeezes at a given stretch — sympathetic stimulation raises it. Afterload is the pressure it must overcome to open the aortic valve and eject. Raise afterload, as chronic hypertension does, and the ventricle must work harder for every beat — which is why untreated hypertension eventually thickens and then fails the left ventricle.

Why the heart cannot cramp

Skeletal muscle can be stimulated repeatedly until the twitches fuse into one sustained contraction — tetanus. If the heart could do that, it would seize and never refill, and you would die.

It cannot, because cardiac muscle has an unusually long refractory period. The plateau phase — sustained by calcium entering while potassium leaves — keeps the cell unresponsive until the contraction is over and relaxation has begun. The heart is physically incapable of a second beat before the first has finished. That constraint is a safety feature.

The two heart sounds, and which valves make them

Put a stethoscope on a chest and you hear lub-dub: two sounds, four valves.

The first sound, S1, is the atrioventricular valves slamming shut — the tricuspid on the right, the mitral on the left. They close the moment ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, so S1 marks the start of systole. The second sound, S2, is the semilunar valves — pulmonary and aortic — snapping shut as the ventricles relax and arterial pressure pushes blood back toward them, marking the start of diastole.

No valve is pulled open or pushed shut by muscle — every one moves on a pressure difference alone, and the papillary muscles only anchor the AV valves so they cannot turn inside out. Which is why a stiff or leaking valve gives the turbulent flow you hear as a murmur.

Reading the ECG wave by wave

An ECG records electrical events, not contractions — each wave is a voltage change that a mechanical event follows.

The P wave is atrial depolarization. The flat PR segment after it is the signal being held at the AV node, giving the atria time to finish emptying. The QRS complex is ventricular depolarization — large, because the ventricles carry far more muscle. Atrial repolarization happens at the same moment, buried inside the QRS, which is why you never see a wave for it. The ST segment should sit flat during contraction, and the T wave is ventricular repolarization.

That flat segment matters clinically: starved of oxygen, myocardium shifts it — depressed in ischemia, elevated when muscle is injuring and infarcting — which is how a blocked coronary announces itself on the monitor.

Why the heart beats itself

A heart removed from the body keeps beating, and a transplanted heart beats before any nerve reaches it. That is because pacemaker cells have no stable resting potential — sodium leaks inward through funny channels until the cell drifts to threshold and fires on its own. That drift is the heartbeat.

Every part of the conduction system can do this, at different speeds. The SA node drifts fastest — around 100 a minute on its own, 60 to 100 with vagal braking — so it sets the rhythm; the AV node manages 40 to 60, and the bundle of His, bundle branches and Purkinje fibers only 20 to 40.

The fastest pacemaker wins. Which is why, if the SA node fails, a slower site takes over — the patient goes bradycardic, but stays alive.

Why your resting pulse is not 100

If the SA node fires at around 100 on its own, why is your resting pulse nearer 70? Because the vagus nerve brakes it continuously. That standing parasympathetic traffic — vagal tone — releases acetylcholine onto the node, slows the drift to threshold and holds you below your own intrinsic rate.

Sympathetic fibers release norepinephrine, and the adrenal medulla adds circulating epinephrine — both doing the opposite, through three effects: chronotropy (rate at the SA node), dromotropy (conduction speed at the AV node) and inotropy (force of contraction). Parasympathetic supply reaches the nodes but barely touches ventricular muscle, so the vagus changes rate far more than force.

When you stand and your pulse jumps, the first thing that happened was vagal withdrawal — releasing the brake is quicker than pressing the accelerator.

The heart feeds itself during diastole

The heart sits in a chamber full of blood and cannot use a drop of it. Myocardium is fed by the coronary arteries, which branch off the aorta just above the aortic valve — the first vessels off the pump.

Here is the catch. During systole the contracting left ventricle squeezes its own vessels almost shut, so the coronaries fill during diastole, when the muscle is relaxed and the closed aortic valve holds a column of pressurized blood above them. Left-heart perfusion is a diastolic event.

Now think about tachycardia. As rate climbs, diastole shortens far more than systole — a racing heart demands more oxygen while giving itself less time to collect any. Which is why chest pain appears on exertion, and why slowing the rate is so often the treatment.

Two immune systems, working together

Innate immunity is what you are born with: fast, non-specific, no memory. Skin and mucous membranes, neutrophils and macrophages, NK cells, complement, inflammation and fever. It responds in minutes but treats every intruder the same.

Adaptive immunity is specific and remembers. It takes days on first exposure, but the second time it is dramatically faster — which is the entire principle behind vaccination.

NK cells are the confusing case: they are lymphocytes, which feels adaptive, but they attack without prior exposure, so they are innate.

Who does what in the adaptive response

Helper T cells (CD4) coordinate. They activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells and release cytokines — which is precisely why HIV, by destroying them, disables both arms at once.

B cells recognize antigen and become plasma cells, which mass-produce antibody. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8) kill infected cells directly. Memory cells of both kinds persist afterwards, and are the reason the second exposure is so much easier.

The lymphatic system does two jobs

First, immunity: lymph nodes filter lymph and hold lymphocytes and macrophages, the spleen filters blood and clears old red cells, the thymus matures T cells, and MALT guards mucosal surfaces.

Second, and easy to forget: fluid balance. Capillaries leak fluid into tissue continuously. The lymphatics collect that excess and return it to the bloodstream at the subclavian veins. Block the drainage and the fluid has nowhere to go — that is lymphedema.

Antibodies, and which one is where

All five immunoglobulins do the same basic thing — bind antigen — but each is deployed differently.

IgM is produced first in a new infection, so finding it suggests a current or recent one. IgG is the most abundant, arrives later, and is the only one that crosses the placenta — which is how a newborn borrows its mother's immunity for a few months. IgA guards mucosal surfaces and is in breast milk. IgE binds mast cells and drives allergy and parasite defense. IgD sits on B cells as a receptor.

If a question mentions a newborn, think IgG. Mucosa or milk, think IgA. Allergy, think IgE.

Active and passive immunity

Active immunity means you made the response yourself — through infection or vaccination. It is slow to establish but leaves memory, so it lasts.

Passive immunity means someone else's antibodies were handed to you — across the placenta, in breast milk, or as an injection. Protection is immediate, which is exactly why it is used in emergencies, but it fades within weeks to months and leaves no memory, because your own B cells were never involved.

Immediate but temporary, or slow but lasting. That trade-off is the whole distinction.

The complement system, all three pathways

Complement is a set of plasma proteins that circulate inactive until one of three pathways switches them on. The classical pathway starts when C1 binds an antibody already stuck to an antigen. The lectin pathway starts when mannose-binding lectin recognizes sugars on microbes but not on yours. The alternative pathway starts by itself, when activated C3 lands on a foreign surface lacking your regulatory proteins.

All three converge on one cascade with three outcomes. C3b coats the microbe so phagocytes can grip it — opsonization. C3a and C5a drive inflammation, and C5a especially pulls neutrophils in. C5b through C9 assemble the membrane attack complex, a pore that lyses the cell. Which is why people missing late complement proteins get repeated Neisseria infections — antibody alone cannot drill that hole.

The innate arsenal, layer by layer

Innate defense is layered, and a microbe meets those layers in order. The barriers come first — intact skin, mucus and cilia sweeping the airway, stomach acid, lysozyme in tears and saliva, and normal flora holding the space.

Break through those and cells respond. Neutrophils arrive first and phagocytose. Macrophages engulf and present what they find. Dendritic cells carry antigen to the lymph nodes, where adaptive immunity begins. Eosinophils attack parasites, while basophils and mast cells release histamine. Natural killer cells are lymphocytes, but innate ones — they kill any cell that has stopped displaying normal self markers, without ever having met it before. Interferons, complement, inflammation and fever run alongside. None of it requires prior exposure, which is why it works in minutes rather than days.

MHC, and why CD4 and CD8 see different things

A T cell cannot see free antigen — only fragments displayed on a major histocompatibility complex molecule — and the two classes explain the division of labor. MHC class I sits on every nucleated cell, displaying proteins made inside it. If a virus replicates there, its fragments appear on the surface, and CD8 cytotoxic T cells read class I and kill the cell outright. MHC class II sits only on antigen-presenting cells — dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells — displaying material swallowed from outside. CD4 helper T cells read class II and release cytokines that activate B cells, cytotoxic T cells and macrophages.

Which is why HIV is so devastating: it destroys CD4 cells, and without the coordinator, otherwise healthy B cells and CD8 cells never get switched on.

Why the second exposure is different

The first time you meet an antigen the response is slow — several days while the few lymphocytes that happen to fit divide into a clone. Antibody appears late, at a low level, and it is mostly IgM. That lag is why a first infection makes you ill.

Some of that clone becomes memory B and T cells, which persist for years. On re-exposure the secondary response starts within hours, climbs far higher, lasts longer and is mostly IgG. You often clear the organism before symptoms begin. That is exactly what a vaccine buys, and why boosters exist — each repeat exposure enlarges the memory pool. It is also why a laboratory finding of IgM says recent infection while IgG says past infection or vaccination.

When tolerance fails

Lymphocytes are built with random receptors, so some inevitably recognize you. Tolerance is the screening that removes them. T cells are tested in the thymus: those that cannot read your MHC die, and those that bind your own proteins too strongly are deleted. B cells are screened separately in bone marrow, where those binding your tissue are deleted or made to rebuild their receptor.

When that screening fails you get autoimmunity — Hashimoto thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, lupus. None of these is weakness. The system is working at full strength and simply aiming at the wrong target, which is why the treatment is immunosuppression rather than immune stimulation — the opposite of what you give in immunodeficiency, where the response is too small rather than misdirected.

Breathing is a pressure problem

Air moves down a pressure gradient, and your diaphragm creates that gradient. On inhalation the diaphragm contracts and flattens, the thoracic cavity gets bigger, and by Boyle's law a bigger space means lower pressure — so pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric and air flows in. On quiet exhalation the diaphragm relaxes, the cavity shrinks, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out. Quiet exhalation is passive; it costs nothing but elastic recoil.

Surfactant, made by Type II alveolar cells, lowers surface tension so alveoli do not collapse on themselves. A premature infant without it has to fight to reinflate the lungs with every breath.

Ventilation, perfusion, diffusion

Three separate requirements, and all three must work. Ventilation is air reaching the alveoli. Perfusion is blood reaching the capillaries around them. Diffusion is gas actually crossing the membrane, driven by the partial pressure gradient.

A pulmonary embolism ventilates fine but does not perfuse. Pulmonary edema perfuses fine but the membrane is too thick to diffuse across. Naming which of the three has failed is usually the whole question.

How the gases travel

Oxygen is barely soluble, so about 98% rides on hemoglobin and only a couple of percent dissolves. Carbon dioxide is different: roughly 70% travels as bicarbonate in the plasma, about 23% on hemoglobin, and the rest dissolved.

That bicarbonate route is why respiration and pH are inseparable. CO₂ plus water makes carbonic acid, which releases H⁺. Retain CO₂ and you become acidotic; blow it off and you become alkalotic. The lungs are a pH organ as much as a gas organ.

Lung volumes, and why some air never leaves

A normal quiet breath is tidal volume, roughly 500 mL. You can inhale a lot more on top of it and exhale a lot more beyond it, and together with tidal volume that maximum sweep is vital capacity.

But no matter how hard you blow out, some air stays — residual volume. That is not a flaw. It keeps alveoli open between breaths so they do not have to be reinflated from collapsed each time, and it means gas exchange continues even while you are exhaling.

Compliance: how easily lungs inflate

Compliance is how readily the lungs expand for a given effort. Surfactant raises it by cutting surface tension. Scarring lowers it — in pulmonary fibrosis the lungs are stiff, and every breath costs more work.

Compliance and airway obstruction are different problems and it is worth keeping them apart. Fibrosis makes lungs hard to inflate. COPD makes air hard to get out, because narrowed airways and lost elastic recoil trap it on exhalation. Restrictive versus obstructive, in one sentence each.

Which muscles do the work of breathing

Quiet inspiration is the only part of normal breathing that costs muscular energy. The diaphragm flattens and the external intercostals lift the ribs outward, the thorax enlarges, and air follows the pressure drop inward. The diaphragm alone does about 70 percent of that work.

Quiet expiration is passive. You simply stop contracting and the stretched elastic tissue of the lungs and chest wall recoils — no muscle needed. Only forced expiration recruits the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles, and only forced inspiration calls in the scalenes, sternocleidomastoid and pectoralis minor.

Which is why a patient using neck muscles to pull air in, or belly muscles to push it out, is telling you the effortless version of breathing has already failed.

The three cells lining an alveolus

An alveolus has three cell types, and only one of them exchanges gas.

Type I alveolar cells are flat squamous cells covering about 95 percent of the surface. Being thin is the entire point — oxygen and carbon dioxide cross them almost instantly. Type II cells are cuboidal, occupy the rest, and secrete surfactant, the detergent-like film that lowers surface tension so an alveolus does not collapse on itself. They also serve as the repair stem cell after lung injury. Alveolar macrophages, or dust cells, patrol the surface and engulf whatever was inhaled past the airways.

Surfactant does not reach adequate levels until roughly week 34 of gestation, which is why a premature infant's alveoli collapse at every breath out and must be reopened from nothing — neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

How hemoglobin decides when to let go

Each hemoglobin molecule carries four oxygen molecules, and binding the first makes the next easier — cooperative binding. That is why the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve is S-shaped: in the lungs saturation holds near 98 percent even as the partial pressure dips, while in the tissues the curve falls steeply, so a small pressure drop unloads a lot of oxygen.

Four things shift the curve right, so hemoglobin releases oxygen more readily: rising carbon dioxide, falling pH, rising temperature and rising 2,3-BPG. The carbon dioxide and pH half of that is the Bohr effect. The opposite conditions shift it left and hold oxygen on.

Which is why working muscle — hot, acidic and loaded with carbon dioxide — strips far more oxygen from the same arterial blood than resting tissue does.

Carbon dioxide travels as bicarbonate

Oxygen and carbon dioxide do not travel the same way. About 70 percent of carbon dioxide is carried as bicarbonate, 20 percent rides on the globin part of hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin, and only 7 to 10 percent dissolves in plasma.

That bicarbonate is made inside the red cell: carbonic anhydrase joins carbon dioxide to water, forming carbonic acid, which splits into a hydrogen ion and bicarbonate. The bicarbonate leaves for the plasma and a chloride ion moves in to balance the charge — the chloride shift. In the lung the reaction reverses and the gas is blown off.

Because that reaction makes acid, ventilation sets pH — hypoventilating traps carbon dioxide and drops the pH, while hyperventilating blows it off, which is why a panicking patient who over-breathes goes alkalotic and tingles.

When ventilation and perfusion do not match

Air and blood must arrive at the same alveolus for exchange to happen, and the ventilation to perfusion ratio is about 0.8. There are two ways to break it.

A shunt is perfusion without ventilation — blood flows past an alveolus with no air, as in pneumonia, atelectasis or pulmonary edema. That blood reaches the left heart still deoxygenated, and extra oxygen helps little because air cannot reach it. Dead space is the mirror image: ventilation without perfusion, classically a pulmonary embolism blocking flow to an alveolus that is perfectly well ventilated.

The lung defends itself with hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction: vessels serving poorly ventilated regions narrow, pushing blood toward alveoli that actually have air. Which is why a small mismatch stays invisible while a large one drops saturation fast.

Every misconception on this exam

  • "S1 is the semilunar valves." S1 = AV valves closing. S2 = semilunar valves closing.
  • "Cardiac output is just heart rate." CO = HR × stroke volume. Both matter.
  • "The QRS shows atrial repolarization." It shows ventricular depolarization; atrial repolarization is hidden inside it.
  • "NK cells are adaptive." They are lymphocytes but act without prior exposure — innate.
  • "Passive immunity gives lasting protection." It fades in weeks to months and leaves no memory.
  • "Autoimmunity means a weak immune system." It is misdirected, not weak.
  • "Exhalation is active." Quiet exhalation is passive elastic recoil.
  • "CO₂ travels mostly on hemoglobin." ~70% travels as bicarbonate in plasma.
  • "Type II cells perform gas exchange." Type I exchange gas; Type II make surfactant.
