High-yield facts

  • Simple squamous = diffusion/filtration. Stratified squamous = protection. Transitional = stretch.
  • Epidermal layers deep→superficial: basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum, corneum.
  • The epidermis is avascular; the dermis carries the vessels and nerves.
  • Vitamin D: UVB on skin → liver (calcidiol) → kidney (calcitriol, active).

Topic walkthrough — read this first

Epithelium: shape tells you the job

Epithelium covers surfaces and lines cavities, and its shape is a direct clue to its function. Squamous cells are flat — thin means fast diffusion, which is why alveoli and capillaries use them. Cuboidal cells are boxy with room for machinery, so they secrete and absorb, as in kidney tubules. Columnar cells are tall with even more room, lining the digestive tract for absorption.

Then layers: simple means one layer and favors exchange; stratified means many layers and favors protection, which is why skin and esophagus are stratified squamous. Transitional epithelium changes shape as it stretches, which only the bladder and ureters need. Pseudostratified looks layered but is not, and in the airway it comes with cilia and goblet cells to trap and sweep debris.

Every epithelium is avascular. It is fed by diffusion from the connective tissue beneath, which is why the outermost skin cells eventually die.

Connective tissue is mostly matrix

Where epithelium is packed with cells, connective tissue is mostly the matrix between them, and the matrix determines the property. Dense collagen in parallel bundles gives one-directional tensile strength — tendons. Collagen in every direction gives strength from all sides — the dermis. Elastic fibers let a structure spring back — the ear. Mineralized matrix gives hardness — bone. And a liquid matrix gives transport — blood and lymph, which are connective tissues too, however odd that sounds at first.

Skin as an organ with a job list

The epidermis is the barrier. Cells are born in the stratum basale, and as they are pushed outward they fill with keratin, flatten, and die, ending as the stratum corneum — a sheet of tough, waterproof dead cells. That is not a design flaw; a dead, keratin-packed surface is exactly what resists water loss and blocks pathogens.

The dermis underneath carries the blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands. Its vessels also do thermoregulation: dilate to dump heat, constrict to keep it. The hypodermis below is fat — insulation, cushioning, anchoring.

Melanin and vitamin D — the same UV, two opposite stories

Melanin, made by melanocytes in the basale, absorbs ultraviolet light before it can damage DNA. More melanin means more protection from UV.

But the skin also needs UV. UVB converts a cholesterol precursor into vitamin D3, the liver converts that to calcidiol, and the kidney makes the active form, calcitriol. Calcitriol's job is to increase calcium absorption from the gut — which is why a skin topic ends up controlling your bones, and why chronic kidney disease weakens the skeleton.

What holds epithelium together

Every epithelium is polarized — an apical surface facing the lumen or the outside world, and a basal surface anchored to a basement membrane. That anchoring matters, because epithelium is avascular: every nutrient must diffuse up from the connective tissue beneath.

Five junction types hold the sheet together. Tight junctions ring the cells near the apex and control what passes between them, keeping the lumen chemically separate from the tissue below. Adherens junctions and desmosomes rivet neighbors against mechanical stress, and hemidesmosomes pin the bottom row to the basement membrane. Gap junctions leave pores open so ions pass straight from cell to cell. And because epithelium divides constantly to replace what it loses, every division is another chance for a mutation — which is why most adult cancers are carcinomas.

The four body membranes

A body membrane is a sheet of epithelium plus the connective tissue under it — with one deliberate exception.

Mucous membranes line every passage open to the outside — respiratory, digestive, urinary, reproductive — and stay wet, mostly with mucus that traps debris and keeps the surface from drying. Serous membranes line closed cavities and wrap the organs inside — pleura, pericardium, peritoneum — each with a parietal layer on the wall, a visceral layer on the organ, and a film of serous fluid between so the two glide. The cutaneous membrane is the skin, and the only dry one. Synovial membranes line joint cavities and contain no epithelium — connective tissue only, the exception. Inflame a serous membrane and those layers roughen and grate — which is why pericarditis rubs and hurts with every heartbeat.

Walking up the epidermis

Start at the bottom. The stratum basale is where nearly all division happens, pushing daughters upward, and it houses Merkel cells and the melanocytes that hand pigment to keratinocytes to cap their nuclei; melanin is the shield against UV, not the cause of damage. The stratum spinosum is packed with desmosomes and carries immune Langerhans cells. In the stratum granulosum the cells fill with keratohyalin, waterproof themselves, and die. The stratum lucidum appears only in thick skin — palms and soles. The stratum corneum is twenty to thirty layers of dead, keratin-filled cells.

The epidermis has no vessels of its own — every nutrient diffuses up from dermal capillaries — which is why a burn that strips the basale cannot regrow from that layer and must resurface from follicles left in the dermis.

What the dermis carries

Two layers, two jobs. The papillary layer is loose areolar tissue thrown into dermal papillae that push up into the epidermis, carrying the capillary loops that feed it, the Meissner corpuscles that read light touch, and — on your fingertips — the ridges that make fingerprints. The reticular layer beneath is dense irregular connective tissue — interwoven collagen for strength, elastin for recoil — and it is the address for hair follicles, sweat and sebaceous glands, blood vessels, nerves, and deep-pressure Pacinian corpuscles.

Those follicles and glands are lined with epidermal cells, which is how burn depth is judged. A partial-thickness burn spares them, so new epithelium creeps outward from each until the surface closes. A full-thickness burn destroys them — which is why it has no source of new skin and needs a graft.

Glands, and the three ways they let go

Skin glands are named for how the cell releases its product, and only one of the three leaves the cell intact. Merocrine glands package the product into vesicles and let it out by exocytosis, losing nothing — the eccrine sweat glands that cool you by evaporation work this way, as do salivary and pancreatic glands.

Apocrine glands pinch off the apical part of the cell with the secretion; they empty into hair follicles in the axilla and groin, and ceruminous and mammary glands are counted with them. Their sweat is odorless until skin bacteria digest it — that is body odor. Holocrine glands rupture the whole cell — sebaceous glands do this, so sebum is lipid plus cell debris, which is why a plugged follicle keeps filling, inflames, and becomes acne.

Common misconceptions

  • "The epidermis has its own blood supply." It is avascular — fed by diffusion from the dermis. That is why its outer cells die.
  • "Vitamin D is fully made in the skin." Skin only starts it. The liver then the kidney complete activation to calcitriol.
  • "Melanin causes skin cancer." Melanin protects against it by absorbing UV.
