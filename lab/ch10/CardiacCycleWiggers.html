The Cardiac Cycle

Cardiac valves have no muscle and no nerve supply. Each opens when the pressure upstream exceeds the pressure downstream and shuts when the gradient reverses — so the whole cycle can be derived rather than memorized. Drag the playhead through one beat and watch each valve flip at the exact moment two curves cross, while the same beat draws itself as a pressure–volume loop.

The same beat as a pressure–volume loop

The loop runs counter-clockwise. Bottom edge moving right is filling; the right vertical edge is isovolumetric contraction; the top edge moving left is ejection; the left vertical edge is isovolumetric relaxation. The width of the loop is stroke volume, and the area inside it is stroke work — the external work the ventricle performs each beat.

Two things worth testing here

Ejection fraction is not stroke volume. Set EDV to 60 mL and ESV to 24 mL. The ejection fraction reads 60 percent — reassuring — yet stroke volume is only 36 mL. Roughly half of all heart failure occurs with preserved ejection fraction: the ventricle is stiff, it fills poorly, EDV is low, and output falls even though the percentage ejected looks normal.
Tachycardia steals diastole. Drag the heart rate to 190 and watch the diastole-per-minute figure. Left ventricular coronary flow happens almost entirely in diastole, because systolic compression of the intramural vessels nearly abolishes it. Perfusion time therefore falls at exactly the moment oxygen demand is highest — and since the myocardium already extracts 70 to 80 percent of the oxygen delivered to it, it cannot compensate by extracting more.
