Two Pumps in Series

Both ventricles move the same 5 L/min, because the circuit is closed and they sit one after the other. What differs is the pressure each must build, and that is set entirely by the resistance downstream: ΔP = Q × R. Change each circuit’s resistance below and watch the required pressures move. Then rebuild a ventricular wall and read the stress each sarcomere carries from T = P × r / 2h.

The circuit: fix the flow, change the resistance

Resistance units here are mmHg per L/min. Multiply by 80 for the dyn·s·cm−5 you will see on a hemodynamic report. Normal systemic is about 18 (1440 dyn·s·cm−5); normal pulmonary is about 1.8 (144 dyn·s·cm−5) — one tenth as much, which is the whole explanation for the pressure difference.

Wall geometry: T = P × r / 2h

Try a ventricle
Normal free wall thickness: left ventricle 8–12 mm, right ventricle 3–5 mm. Wall stress is what each individual sarcomere bears, and it is the main determinant of how much oxygen the myocardium consumes.

The misconception this retires

Push the systemic resistance slider up and the pulmonary one down as far as they go. The left ventricle now generates several times the right ventricle’s pressure — and the flow readout never moves. The left ventricle is stronger because it works against more resistance, not because it moves more blood. In a closed series circuit the two outputs must be equal; a sustained mismatch of even 1–2 mL per beat would flood one circuit within minutes.
