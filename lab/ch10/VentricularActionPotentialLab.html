The Ventricular Action Potential

A working ventricular myocyte rests at −90 mV and, once triggered, spends 200–300 ms depolarized because inward calcium balances outward potassium through a long plateau. Reshape that plateau below, change the calcium that reaches troponin, then fire a test stimulus anywhere on the trace and see for yourself where the cell refuses to answer — which is why cardiac muscle cannot be tetanized.

Phase 0 is one of physiology’s few genuine positive feedback loops — depolarization opens sodium channels, which causes more depolarization. It is tolerated only because sodium channel inactivation shuts it down within milliseconds.

Why the plateau is the point

Drag the plateau down to 60 ms and the shaded refractory band shrinks with it — the cell can now be re-excited long before it has relaxed, and twitches would fuse. A tetanized ventricle is a ventricle that never fills, and a heart that never fills produces no output. The plateau is the design feature that guarantees contraction and relaxation alternate.
The other direction is dangerous too. Blocking IKr — or simply being hypokalemic — drags phase 3 out, lengthens the action potential and, on the surface ECG, the QT interval (normal corrected QT under about 440 ms). That is the setting for the polymorphic ventricular tachycardia called torsades de pointes, and it is one of the commonest reasons a drug is pulled from a formulary.
