Starling Forces and the Lymphatic Drain

Across 24 hours a normal body filters about 20 liters of fluid out of its capillaries and pulls only about 17 liters back, because the albumin that escapes cannot diffuse home against its own gradient. The missing 3 liters must leave by lymph. Set the six Starling terms, set the pump that moves lymph, then run the tissue forward in time and watch it either balance or fill.

The six Starling terms

πc is held up by albumin (normal plasma 3.5–5.0 g/dL). Below about 2.5 g/dL the oncotic pull collapses far enough to flood every capillary bed at once. Pi and πi are not sliders here — they are outputs, because the tissue itself sets them as it fills with water and stranded protein.

The drain: what actually moves lymph

A lymphangion is the segment between two valves. At rest it contracts 6–10 times a minute at 5–20 mmHg a beat, and stretch raises both rate (to about 30/min) and force — a Starling’s-law-of-the-heart analog that auto-matches output to load. Every mechanism here needs competent valves: set valve competence to zero and the pump becomes a sloshing reservoir.

The pale band marks 2.5–3 liters of excess — roughly where edema first becomes palpable and where body weight has risen a few kilograms. Watch πi as well as volume: if the drain fails, stranded protein raises πi, which weakens the inward oncotic pull, which increases filtration. That is the self-reinforcing spiral the lymphatics exist to break.
