Grading Force

A single fiber barely grades its own force: its action potential is all-or-none and its calcium release is close to saturating. Whole-muscle force is graded in two other ways — by recruiting motor units in a fixed small-to-large order, and by firing faster until twitches fuse. A third factor, filament overlap, caps what either can achieve. Then something has to pay for it.
Which muscle are you driving?
Why smallest first is automatic. Small motor neurons have a higher input resistance, so the same synaptic current depolarizes them more — V = I × R rearranged. They therefore reach threshold first, and conveniently they innervate small, slow, fatigue-resistant fibers. The order does not change with willpower or with training; only the firing rate and the endpoint change.

The mismatch that makes it possible. The muscle action potential is over in 1 to 5 ms, but the mechanical twitch lasts tens of milliseconds — about 20 ms in a fast fiber and up to 100 ms in a slow one. So a second impulse can arrive while calcium is still elevated and tension is still present. Watch the calcium trace underneath: SERCA simply cannot clear it between stimuli.
Clinically useful points on the curve
The curve is pure geometry. Active tension is a direct readout of how many cross-bridges can form. Optimal length is 2.0 to 2.2 µm. Stretch past about 3.6 µm and overlap is zero. Compress below 2.0 µm and thin filaments from opposite ends interfere with each other; below about 1.7 µm the thick filaments jam against the Z discs and tension falls steeply.
How hard is the work?
The stores are tiny. A resting fiber holds only about 5 mmol of ATP per kilogram — enough for perhaps 2 to 3 seconds of maximal work. Creatine phosphate at 20 to 25 mmol/kg covers the first 10 to 15 seconds. Anaerobic glycolysis yields 2 ATP per glucose and sustains intense work for 30 seconds to 2 minutes. Oxidative phosphorylation yields about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose but takes 30 to 60 seconds to ramp up.
