Starling Forces and the Interstitial Compartment

The interstitium is not a puddle — its volume is the steady-state answer to four pressures and a reflection coefficient, with the lymphatics as the third term made anatomical. Set the forces below and the simulation runs forward in time: fluid accumulates or drains until the books balance. Push one force far enough and you will watch edema appear, then pit.

Model notes. Pc is taken to fall linearly along the capillary. Whole-body constants are calibrated so that the normal state reproduces the textbook budget: about 20 L/day filtered, 17 L/day reabsorbed, and 3 L/day handed to the lymphatics along with 100–200 g of escaped plasma protein. Interstitial compliance is low while the fluid is held in the proteoglycan gel and high once free fluid appears; lymph flow rises steeply with interstitial pressure and then saturates.

Misconception. Pitting edema is not simply “water in the tissue.” It is expansion of the interstitial volume beyond what the proteoglycan gel can hold, so that free fluid appears and a thumb can displace it. Interstitial pressure must climb from about −2 mmHg to slightly positive first — which is why 2–3 L can accumulate before any edema is visible at all.
