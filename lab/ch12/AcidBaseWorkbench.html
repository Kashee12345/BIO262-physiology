The Lung as an Acid–Base Organ

Metabolism makes about 15,000 mmol of CO2 a day, and CO2 hydrates to carbonic acid — so ventilation is a pH control system. Everything reduces to protecting the 20:1 ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO2. Move each limb below and watch pH, the disorder name and the adequacy of compensation change together; then use the A–a gradient to decide whether a low PaO2 is a drive problem or an exchange problem.

Load a blood gas
The division of labor. The lung disposes of the entire volatile acid load in minutes but can only handle one acid. The kidney handles the 50 to 100 mEq of fixed acid a day — sulfuric, phosphoric, lactic, keto — over hours to days, but it can excrete anything and can regenerate bicarbonate. That is why the respiratory limb moves first and the metabolic limb tells you how long this has been going on.

Read in a fixed order. First pH: acidemia or alkalemia. Second PaCO2: if it moves in the direction that would explain the pH, the disorder is respiratory. Third bicarbonate: if it explains the pH, the disorder is metabolic. Fourth, check whether the other limb has moved in the compensating direction and by an appropriate amount. Fifth, if there is a metabolic acidosis, take the anion gap.

Same low PaO2, entirely different physiology and entirely different treatment. A normal gradient means the alveolus itself is oxygen-poor — hypoventilation, or low inspired oxygen as at altitude — and the answer is ventilation or a higher FiO2. A widened gradient means the alveolus is fine but the blood is not picking oxygen up: V/Q mismatch, shunt or diffusion impairment.
