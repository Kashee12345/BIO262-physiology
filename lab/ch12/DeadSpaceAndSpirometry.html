Then blow into a spirometer you control, and separate airways that will not empty from lungs that will not fill.

Anatomical dead space is the roughly 150 mL of conducting airway — nose to terminal bronchioles, generations 0 through 16 — where there is no capillary bed. Alveolar dead space is alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused, near zero in health and large in pulmonary embolism. Push the dead space slider up to model either a long breathing tube or a lung full of unperfused alveoli.

One minute of breathing, drawn

The fixed tax. Dead space is subtracted from every breath, so its cost scales with rate, not with volume. Rapid shallow breathing pays the tax more often on a smaller purchase. That is why a fatiguing patient whose respiratory rate looks reassuringly “compensatory” can be retaining CO2, and why a pulse oximeter reading 96 percent on supplemental oxygen says nothing at all about ventilation.

Residual volume, FRC and TLC cannot be measured with a spirometer — you cannot exhale what will not come out. Those values come from helium dilution, nitrogen washout or body plethysmography. They are shown here because the direction they move is what separates air trapping from a small lung.

The spirogram and the verdict

Why trying harder can make it worse. A forced effort drives pleural pressure strongly positive, compressing airways from the outside. In emphysema the elastic tethers that hold small airways open are gone, so the equal pressure point moves peripherally into floppy airway and those airways collapse — dynamic airway compression. Pursed-lip breathing works because it keeps pressure inside the airway up and pushes that point back toward cartilage.
