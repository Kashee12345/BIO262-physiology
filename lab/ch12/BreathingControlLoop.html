The Control of Breathing

The regulated variable is arterial PCO2, held near 40 mmHg. The sensor is the central chemoreceptor reading H+ in cerebrospinal fluid; the integrator is the medulla; the effector is the diaphragm. Oxygen is only a backup, and it does not engage until PaO2 falls below about 60 mmHg. Run the loop below, disturb it, then break the sensor, the integrator or the effector and watch what a variable does when nothing is watching it.

Break the loop

Click a box to lesion it and watch what the trace does. Note which break is survivable: without central chemoreceptors the peripheral ones still hold PaCO2 somewhere, badly. Without the effector, no amount of sensing helps at all — which is exactly why a cervical cord injury above C3 abolishes spontaneous breathing while a lesion at C6 leaves a patient who breathes but cannot cough forcefully.

The two response curves

Why CO2 and not O2. Hydrogen ions and bicarbonate cross the blood–brain barrier poorly; dissolved CO2 crosses freely, meets carbonic anhydrase in cerebrospinal fluid and generates H+ there. Because CSF has almost no protein buffer, its pH swings far more per unit of CO2 than blood pH does — so a rise of only 1 to 2 mmHg in PaCO2 measurably increases ventilation. Raise CSF bicarbonate and the whole CO2 curve slides to the right: that is adaptation in a chronic retainer, not repair.
