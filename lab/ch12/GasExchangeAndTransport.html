Diffusion, V/Q Matching and Gas Transport

Three separate machines have to work for oxygen to reach a mitochondrion: a curve that loads and unloads it, a lung that puts air and blood in the same place, and a membrane half a micrometer thick. Shift the curve, break the matching, and thicken the membrane below — and watch which failure supplemental oxygen can fix and which one it cannot.

Put air and blood in the same place

Load a V/Q scenario
The rest of the lung is held at a normal V/Q of 0.80, and a fixed 3.5 percent physiological shunt is always present — bronchial and thebesian blood that bypasses alveoli even in health. That small shunt is why a healthy arterial PO2 is 95 mmHg rather than the alveolar 104, and why the normal A–a gradient is not zero.

The lung defends its own matching. When alveolar PO2 falls below about 70 mmHg, pulmonary arterioles constrict — the opposite of every systemic bed — and divert blood toward better-ventilated lung. Elegant regionally, disastrous globally: at altitude or in diffuse chronic lung disease the whole pulmonary bed constricts, pulmonary artery pressure climbs and the right ventricle eventually fails as cor pulmonale.

Race a red cell across the membrane

Fick’s law: transfer rate is proportional to surface area × the partial pressure difference, divided by membrane thickness. A normal membrane is about 0.5 µm thick across roughly 70 m², and a red cell spends about 0.75 s in a pulmonary capillary at rest. Take that safety margin away and see when hypoxemia appears.

Equilibration along the capillary

Carbon dioxide is the control experiment. Its gradient is tiny — 45 mmHg venous to 40 mmHg alveolar — yet it transfers just as completely, because CO2 is about twenty times more soluble in the membrane and so diffuses roughly twenty times faster. That is why diffusion and V/Q problems cause hypoxemia long before they cause hypercapnia, and why a rising PaCO2 nearly always means a ventilation problem rather than an exchange problem.
