Accelerometers and Chemical Codes

The vestibular apparatus splits inertia in two. The semicircular canals are phasic and report angular acceleration, which is why a steady spin stops feeling like anything; the otolith organs are tonic and report gravity, which never stops. Spin a head below and watch the cupula bend, decay and rebound while the vestibulo-ocular reflex counter-rotates the eyes. Then tilt an otolith, and read an odor off a pattern of glomeruli.
Spin the chair
The two moments that matter. Run the rotation and watch the firing rates during the constant speed phase — they return to baseline within 20–30 seconds even though you are still spinning. Then watch what happens the instant the chair stops. The fluid keeps moving, the cupula bends the other way, and you feel violent rotation while sitting still.
Tilt the head, or accelerate it
The comparison to make. Set a tilt and hold it. The otolith afferent steps up and stays up — gravity does not stop, so the receptor must not adapt. The canal afferent beside it fires a burst and then dies back to baseline within 20–30 seconds. Same head, same maneuver, opposite adaptation.
Present an odorant
Knock out one receptor gene
Why 400 genes can discriminate far more than 400 smells. Every neuron expresses essentially one receptor type, and every odorant activates a particular subset to different degrees. Identity is the pattern, not the line — so knock out a receptor and most odors are still recognizable, just slightly different.
Put something on the tongue
Two mechanisms, five qualities. Salty and sour work through ion channels directly, with no second messenger at all. Sweet, umami and bitter work through G-protein-coupled receptors, gustducin and phospholipase C-beta-2. Watch how far apart the thresholds sit — and ask why bitter is the one set a thousand-fold lower.
