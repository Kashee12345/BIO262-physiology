From Pressure Wave to Firing Rate

Airborne sound striking a fluid-filled cochlea would lose about 30 dB at the boundary, so the middle ear buys it back mechanically with an area ratio and a lever. Inside, frequency is sorted before any neuron fires, by a stiffness gradient along the basilar membrane. Rebuild all three stages below: the pressure gain, the traveling wave, and the hair cell running on a +80 mV potassium battery.
Middle ear state
The two multipliers
The acoustic reflex
Try breaking it. Drop the eardrum area to 20 mm² — a perforation — and watch the gain fall and a conductive loss appear, with a perfectly healthy cochlea sitting behind it. Then pick the gunshot and see why a reflex with a 50–100 ms latency cannot protect you from impulse noise.
Play a pure tone
The point of the bench. Sweep the frequency and watch the peak of the traveling wave walk along the membrane. No neuron has fired yet. The cochlea is a bank of tuned filters made of collagen and geometry, and the map it produces is preserved all the way to auditory cortex.
Deflect the stereocilia
The battery
Two experiments. Deflect the bundle negatively and watch firing fall below the resting rate — the hair cell reports in both directions. Then drive the endocochlear potential toward zero, as an aminoglycoside or a high-dose loop diuretic does to the stria vascularis, and watch transduction collapse even though every structure is still present.
