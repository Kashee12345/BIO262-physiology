From Stimulus to Spike Train

Every special sense runs the same three-step machine: an adequate stimulus opens or closes ion channels, that produces a graded receptor potential, and the trigger zone converts amplitude into firing frequency. Drive a stimulus into five different receptors below, hold it there, and watch which ones keep reporting and which ones go quiet. Then use the labeled-line bench to see why pressing on your eyeball produces light.
Choose a receptor
Stimulus
The experiment. Set intensity to 20 — the receptor potential rises but never reaches the −55 mV threshold, so the axon is silent. Now step to 40, 70 and 100 and watch the same spike shape arrive faster and faster. Amplitude never changes. Only interval does.
Deliver an odd stimulus and see what is perceived
Why this matters. An action potential in the optic nerve and one in the cochlear nerve are electrically indistinguishable — roughly 100 mV swings lasting about 1 ms, driven by the same voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channels from Module 6. Nothing in the spike says “light.” The line says light.
Just-noticeable difference
Modality
Sound pressure, in decibels
Weber–Fechner. The just-noticeable difference is a roughly constant fraction of the baseline, not a constant amount. That is why the ear needed a logarithmic unit: dB SPL = 20 × log(P / 20 µPa), where 20 µPa is the threshold of human hearing.
