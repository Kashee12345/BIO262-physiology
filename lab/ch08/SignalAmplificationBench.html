Solubility, Receptor Address and Amplification

One question predicts almost everything about a hormone: is it lipid-soluble? That single property decides whether the receptor sits on the membrane or in the nucleus, whether the answer arrives in seconds or in hours, and whether it stops when the hormone stops. Release a burst of four real hormones here, watch the plasma level decay by its own half-life while the cellular response follows on its own timescale, then build the cascade that turns one binding event into a millionfold answer.

Receptors are the bottleneck. Chronic exposure to a high hormone level causes downregulation — receptors are internalized, so the same plasma concentration produces less effect. That is a central feature of type 2 diabetes and of tolerance to beta-agonists. Chronic low exposure causes upregulation, which is why a long-suppressed tissue can overshoot when stimulation returns.

Specificity is never in the delivery. A neuron drops perhaps 10 000 molecules of acetylcholine into a cleft 20–40 nanometers wide and hits one cell. An endocrine cell empties into a capillary and dilutes into roughly 5 liters of plasma — thyroid hormone circulates near 10−9 molar and many peptides near 10−12 molar. Thyroid hormone bathes your heart and your fingernail at exactly the same concentration; only the heart answers, because only the heart carries dense nuclear T3 receptors. Paracrine and autocrine signals use the same receptors and cascades but skip the bloodstream, so they stay local and brief.
