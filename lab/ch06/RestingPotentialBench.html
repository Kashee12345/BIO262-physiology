The Charged Membrane: Building −70 mV

A resting neuron is a charged capacitor, and the voltage exists only because two conditions hold at once: unequal ion distribution maintained by the Na+/K+ ATPase, and a membrane that is 25–100 times more permeable to K+ than to Na+. Move the concentrations and the permeability ratio and watch Nernst and Goldman answer live — then stop the pump and see why the voltage fades over minutes instead of vanishing the instant the pump dies.

Then press Peak of the action potential and swing it again. That is the module’s rule in one gesture: extracellular K+ sets the resting potential, extracellular Na+ sets the height of the spike.

What the model does. Blocking the pump removes its direct electrogenic contribution of about −4 mV the instant it stops — three positive charges out for every two in is a small outward current, and it disappears at once. Everything after that is the slow part: with no pump to reload them, K+ leaks out and Na+ leaks in and the gradients decay with a time constant of roughly 45 minutes. The permeability ratio stays at 25:1 throughout; only the concentrations change.
The energy bill this represents. The brain is about 2% of body mass and consumes near 20% of resting oxygen uptake, and the majority of a neuron’s ATP goes to running this one pump. That is what you are switching off.
