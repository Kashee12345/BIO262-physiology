1 · What the kidneys actually do

Seven jobs. Filtration is only the first one — the exam likes the others.

FunctionHow
Filtration of bloodRemoves waste, toxins and excess substances from the bloodstream.
Blood volume & pressureBy adjusting how much water is excreted. More water retained → higher blood volume → higher blood pressure.
Electrolyte balanceMaintains sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride and magnesium — needed for nerve transmission and muscle contraction.
Acid–base balanceExcretes hydrogen ions (H⁺) and reabsorbs bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) to hold blood pH at 7.35–7.45.
Excretion of wasteUrea and creatinine leave the body as urine.
Hormone productionErythropoietin → stimulates red blood cell production. Renin → starts the RAAS cascade to raise blood pressure.
DetoxificationExcretes metabolic by-products and drugs.
The one-line rule for electrolytes: water follows sodium. Retain sodium → retain water → blood volume and pressure rise. Excrete sodium → water follows it out → blood volume and pressure fall. Tap a highlighted word to hide it and test yourself.

2 · The nephron, segment by segment

Tap a segment. The whole module hinges on knowing which segment does what to water versus ions.

Urine formation — the three stages

Filtration, reabsorption, secretion. Then excretion carries it out.

StageWhereWhat happens
1 · FiltrationGlomerulus → Bowman's capsulePressure forces water, electrolytes, glucose and small wastes out of the blood into the capsule. The result is filtrate — like plasma but without proteins or blood cells, which are too large to pass.
2 · ReabsorptionMostly PCT, then loop and DCTUseful things are taken back into the blood — water, glucose, amino acids, sodium, chloride. The PCT does the bulk of it.
3 · SecretionDCT and collecting ductExtra waste and ions — H⁺, K⁺, some drugs — are actively pushed from the blood into the filtrate. This is how the kidney fine-tunes pH and potassium.
4 · ExcretionCollecting duct → renal pelvis → ureter → bladderWhat's left is urine: water, urea, electrolytes and waste.

Reabsorption moves substances filtrate → blood. Secretion moves them blood → filtrate. Mixing these two up is the most common error on this material.

3 · RAAS — how the kidney raises blood pressure

A chain reaction that starts when blood pressure drops.

1 · Blood pressure fallsThe kidney senses reduced perfusion.
↓
2 · Kidney releases reninRenin is an enzyme, released from the juxtaglomerular cells.
↓
3 · Angiotensinogen → angiotensin IRenin cleaves angiotensinogen (made by the liver) into angiotensin I.
↓
4 · ACE converts it to angiotensin IIAngiotensin-converting enzyme, largely in the lungs. Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor — that alone raises pressure.
↓
5 · Angiotensin II triggers aldosteroneFrom the adrenal cortex. Aldosterone makes the kidney reabsorb sodium (and therefore water) and excrete potassium.
↓
6 · Blood volume and pressure riseHomeostasis restored — a classic negative feedback loop.
Clinical tie-in: if RAAS is chronically overactive you get hypertension from too much sodium and water retention. That's exactly why ACE inhibitors and ARBs are first-line blood pressure drugs — they interrupt step 4 or block the receptor downstream.

4 · Spermatogenesis

Continuous from puberty onward, in the seminiferous tubules of the testes.

StepCellWhat happens
1Spermatogonium (stem cell, 2n)Divides by mitosis — one daughter stays a stem cell, one commits to becoming sperm. This is why men never run out.
2Primary spermatocyte (2n)Undergoes meiosis I → two secondary spermatocytes.
3Secondary spermatocyte (n)Undergoes meiosis II → two spermatids each.
4Spermatid → spermatozoonSpermiogenesis: no more division, just remodeling into a functional sperm.
The number question: one primary spermatocyte yields four sperm. Contrast that with oogenesis, where one primary oocyte yields one ovum plus polar bodies. This comparison is a guaranteed exam item.

The three parts of a sperm

PartContainsFunction
HeadCondensed nucleus + acrosome capCarries the genetic material. The acrosome holds enzymes that digest through the egg's coating at fertilization.
MidpieceMitochondria, tightly packedProduces the ATP that powers swimming.
Tail (flagellum)MicrotubulesWhips to propel the sperm toward the egg.

Maturation: sperm leaving the seminiferous tubules cannot yet fertilize. They gain motility and egg-binding ability while stored in the epididymis, then travel via the vas deferens and mix with fluid from the seminal vesicles, prostate and bulbourethral glands to form semen.

5 · Oogenesis & follicular development

Unlike sperm, this starts before birth and pauses for years.

StageWhenWhat happens
Fetal lifeBefore birthOogonia multiply and become primary oocytes, which arrest in meiosis I. Every egg a person will ever have is already present.
At birth—Roughly 1–2 million primary oocytes, all still arrested. No new ones are ever made.
Each cycle after pubertyMonthlyFSH rescues a cohort of follicles; usually one becomes dominant. It completes meiosis I, producing a secondary oocyte plus a small polar body.
OvulationDay 14The secondary oocyte is released — and arrests again, in meiosis II.
FertilizationOnly if sperm arrivesMeiosis II completes only if fertilized, producing the mature ovum. No sperm, no completion — the oocyte degenerates.
Ovum vs secondary oocyte — the classic trap: what is ovulated is a secondary oocyte, not an ovum. It only becomes a true ovum once a sperm penetrates it and meiosis II finishes. So strictly speaking, fertilization happens before the egg is finished.

Follicular development

FollicleFeatures
PrimordialPrimary oocyte with a single flat layer of granulosa cells. Dormant until puberty.
PrimaryUnder FSH, granulosa cells become cuboidal and the zona pellucida forms around the oocyte.
SecondaryA theca layer forms, splitting into theca interna (makes hormones) and theca externa (support). Fluid-filled spaces called antra appear.
Tertiary (Graafian)One dominant follicle with a large antrum. Produces high estrogen, which triggers the LH surge → ovulation.
Corpus luteumWhat's left after ovulation. Secretes progesterone to maintain the endometrium. If no pregnancy, it degenerates into the corpus albicans and hormones drop → menstruation.

Zona pellucida = the glycoprotein coat that protects the oocyte and controls sperm entry (it also blocks polyspermy after the first sperm binds).

FSH vs LH — know the difference: FSH grows the follicle. LH releases the egg (and then maintains the corpus luteum). In males: FSH drives sperm production, LH drives testosterone.

7 · Menopause

Typically ages 45–55. Three phases.

PhaseWhat's happening
PerimenopauseBegins years earlier. The ovaries become less responsive to FSH and LH, so estrogen and progesterone fall and fluctuate. Ovulation gets irregular; cycles lengthen, shorten, or are missed.
MenopauseDefined as 12 consecutive months without a period. Estrogen and progesterone are very low, ovulation has stopped entirely, and menstruation no longer occurs.
PostmenopauseHormones stay low and stable. Hot flashes and mood changes usually ease, but the long-term consequences of low estrogen — osteoporosis and increased cardiovascular risk — persist.
The counterintuitive bit that gets tested: in menopause estrogen and progesterone fall — but FSH and LH RISE. The pituitary keeps shouting at ovaries that are no longer answering. Loss of negative feedback, so the signal climbs. Symptoms: hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes, vaginal dryness, bone loss.
